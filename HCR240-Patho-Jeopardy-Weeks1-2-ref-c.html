Hodgkin's Lymphoma

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Malignant transformation of B lymphocytes in a lymph node

Step 2

Reed-Sternberg cells develop (the diagnostic hallmark — giant cells with "owl eye" nuclei)

Step 3

Disease spreads in an orderly fashion to adjacent lymph nodes

Step 4

Constitutional "B symptoms" develop: fever, drenching night sweats, weight loss >10%

Clinical Pearls
  • Reed-Sternberg cells = diagnostic (if you see these on a test, it's Hodgkin's)
  • Painless, rubbery, enlarged lymph nodes (usually cervical)
  • Prior Epstein-Barr virus (EBV/mono) exposure increases risk
  • Bimodal age distribution: young adults (15-35) and older adults (>55)
  • Highly curable with chemotherapy and radiation
Nursing Interventions
  • Monitor for "B symptoms" (fever, night sweats, weight loss)
  • Manage chemotherapy side effects; assess for infection
  • Lymph node assessment at each visit
  • Emotional support — good prognosis, but treatment is intense

Sickle Cell Anemia

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Point mutation in the hemoglobin gene → produces hemoglobin S (HbS) instead of normal HbA

Step 2

Under low O2, dehydration, or stress, HbS polymerizes inside the RBC

Step 3

RBCs distort into rigid crescent (sickle) shapes

Step 4

Sickled cells are sticky and rigid → they clump and block small blood vessels

Step 5

Vaso-occlusive crisis: tissue ischemia → severe pain, organ damage

Step 6

Sickled cells are destroyed prematurely (~20 days vs. normal 120 days) → hemolytic anemia

Clinical Pearls
  • Autosomal recessive — BOTH parents must carry the gene
  • Sickle cell TRAIT (one copy) usually asymptomatic and offers malaria protection
  • Crisis triggers: dehydration, infection, cold, high altitude, stress
  • Increased risk of stroke (especially in children), splenic sequestration, and acute chest syndrome
Nursing Interventions
  • HYDRATION is key — IV and oral fluids to prevent sickling
  • Pain management (often requires opioids during crisis; do not undertreat)
  • Hydroxyurea to reduce frequency of sickling episodes
  • Avoid crisis triggers: keep warm, stay hydrated, prevent infection
  • Folic acid supplementation for increased RBC production

Iron-Deficiency Anemia

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Iron stores become depleted (blood loss, poor intake, or malabsorption)

Step 2

Insufficient iron available for hemoglobin synthesis

Step 3

RBCs are produced that are smaller (microcytic) and paler (hypochromic) than normal

Step 4

Reduced oxygen-carrying capacity → tissue hypoxia

Step 5

Compensatory tachycardia as heart works harder to deliver O2

Clinical Pearls
  • Most common anemia worldwide
  • Pica (craving ice, dirt, starch) is a classic and unique symptom
  • Low serum iron, low ferritin, HIGH TIBC (transferrin is hungry for iron)
  • Common causes: chronic blood loss (GI, menstruation), poor dietary intake
Nursing Interventions
  • Oral ferrous sulfate: take on empty stomach with vitamin C (OJ) to enhance absorption
  • Warn patient: stools will turn black/dark green (this is normal)
  • Use straw for liquid iron (stains teeth)
  • IV iron if oral not tolerated (test dose first, monitor for anaphylaxis)
  • Dietary teaching: red meat, spinach, fortified cereals, beans

Pernicious Anemia

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Autoimmune destruction of parietal cells in the stomach

Step 2

Loss of intrinsic factor (IF) production

Step 3

Without IF, vitamin B12 cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum

Step 4

B12 deficiency impairs DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing cells

Step 5

RBCs become abnormally large (macrocytic/megaloblastic anemia)

Step 6

Neurological damage occurs (B12 is needed for myelin sheath maintenance)

Clinical Pearls
  • No intrinsic factor = no B12 absorption (even if you eat plenty of B12)
  • Neurological symptoms are UNIQUE: paresthesias (tingling), ataxia, memory problems
  • Macrocytic anemia (large RBCs) on CBC with elevated MCV
  • Glossitis (smooth, beefy red tongue) is a classic finding
Nursing Interventions
  • Lifelong B12 injections (IM) since oral B12 cannot be absorbed
  • Assess neurological status: balance, sensation, cognition
  • Fall precautions due to ataxia and paresthesias
  • Teach patient that this is a lifelong condition requiring ongoing treatment

Erythroblastosis Fetalis (Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn)

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Rh-negative mother is exposed to Rh-positive fetal blood (usually during first delivery)

Step 2

Mother's immune system recognizes Rh antigen as foreign → produces anti-Rh antibodies

Step 3

In subsequent pregnancy with an Rh-positive fetus, maternal antibodies cross the placenta

Step 4

Antibodies attack and destroy fetal RBCs (hemolysis)

Step 5

Fetal anemia + massive bilirubin release from RBC breakdown → jaundice

Clinical Pearls
  • Usually doesn't affect the FIRST pregnancy (sensitization occurs during first delivery)
  • Kernicterus (bilirubin crossing blood-brain barrier) is the most feared complication
  • This is a Type II (cytotoxic) hypersensitivity reaction
Nursing Interventions
  • RhoGAM at 28 weeks gestation AND within 72 hours after delivery
  • Phototherapy for newborn jaundice (bili lights break down bilirubin)
  • Monitor bilirubin levels in the newborn
  • Exchange transfusion in severe cases
  • Type and screen ALL pregnant women at first prenatal visit

Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP)

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Often triggered by a recent viral infection (especially in children)

Step 2

Autoantibodies (IgG) are produced against platelet surface glycoproteins

Step 3

Antibody-coated platelets are destroyed by macrophages in the spleen

Step 4

Platelet count drops significantly (<150,000/μL) → impaired hemostasis

Step 5

Spontaneous bleeding, petechiae, purpura, and mucosal bleeding

Clinical Pearls
  • Common post-viral complication in children (often follows chickenpox)
  • Petechiae (pinpoint purple spots) and purpura are hallmarks
  • Platelet count is low, but PT/INR and aPTT are NORMAL (coag factors are fine)
  • Usually self-limiting in children; more chronic in adults
Nursing Interventions
  • Bleeding precautions: soft toothbrush, electric razor, no IM injections
  • Avoid aspirin and NSAIDs (impair remaining platelet function)
  • Corticosteroids (first-line) to suppress immune destruction
  • IVIG for rapid platelet increase in acute bleeding
  • Monitor for signs of internal bleeding (neuro checks for intracranial bleed)

Hemophilia

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

X-linked recessive genetic mutation (primarily affects males)

Step 2

Hemophilia A: deficient factor VIII; Hemophilia B: deficient factor IX

Step 3

Coagulation cascade cannot complete properly

Step 4

Fibrin clot formation is impaired → prolonged bleeding from any injury

Step 5

Spontaneous bleeding into joints (hemarthrosis), muscles, and organs

Clinical Pearls
  • Hemophilia A (factor VIII) is more common than Hemophilia B (factor IX)
  • aPTT is PROLONGED; PT/INR is normal; platelet count is normal
  • Hemarthrosis (bleeding into joints) is the hallmark — causes chronic joint damage
  • Severity depends on factor level: <1% = severe, 1-5% = moderate, 5-40% = mild
Nursing Interventions
  • Replace missing clotting factor (recombinant factor VIII or IX)
  • Desmopressin (DDAVP) for mild Hemophilia A (stimulates factor VIII release)
  • AVOID contact sports, aspirin, and NSAIDs
  • Apply pressure to bleeding sites; ice for joint bleeds (RICE)
  • Teach self-infusion of factor at home for prophylaxis

Atherosclerosis

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Endothelial injury from hypertension, smoking, hyperlipidemia, or diabetes

Step 2

LDL cholesterol infiltrates the damaged arterial wall

Step 3

Macrophages engulf LDL → become "foam cells" (fatty streak)

Step 4

Smooth muscle cells migrate and form a fibrous cap over the lipid core

Step 5

Plaque narrows the artery → reduced blood flow → ischemia downstream

Step 6

Plaque rupture can trigger thrombosis → acute MI or stroke

Clinical Pearls
  • Atherosclerosis is the underlying cause of most heart attacks and strokes
  • Modifiable risk factors: smoking, high LDL, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, sedentary lifestyle
  • Can lead to angina pectoris (stable chest pain with exertion)
  • Statins are first-line treatment for lowering LDL cholesterol
Nursing Interventions
  • Lifestyle modification: DASH diet, exercise 150 min/week, smoking cessation
  • Administer statins; monitor liver function (LFTs)
  • Monitor lipid panel regularly
  • Teach warning signs of MI and stroke (FAST)

Hypertension

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Sustained increase in systemic vascular resistance (arterioles constrict)

Step 2

Heart must pump harder against increased resistance

Step 3

Left ventricle hypertrophies from chronic overwork

Step 4

Chronic pressure damages arterial walls → accelerates atherosclerosis

Step 5

End-organ damage: heart failure, stroke, kidney disease, retinopathy

Clinical Pearls
  • "Silent killer" — usually asymptomatic until organ damage occurs
  • Defined as persistent BP ≥140/90 mmHg (or ≥130/80 per newer guidelines)
  • Primary (essential) hypertension = 90-95% of cases, no identifiable cause
  • Target organs: heart, brain, kidneys, eyes, blood vessels
Nursing Interventions
  • Lifestyle first: DASH diet, sodium restriction (<2300 mg/day), weight loss, exercise
  • Medications: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics
  • Teach proper BP self-monitoring at home
  • Emphasize medication adherence (feeling fine ≠ BP is fine)

Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Virchow's Triad: venous stasis + endothelial injury + hypercoagulability

Step 2

Blood flow slows in deep veins (immobility, post-surgery, long flights)

Step 3

Platelets aggregate at the site → coagulation cascade activates

Step 4

Thrombus (clot) forms in deep vein, usually in the legs

Step 5

Clot can dislodge → travels to lungs → PULMONARY EMBOLISM (life-threatening)

Clinical Pearls
  • Virchow's Triad: Stasis, Endothelial injury, Hypercoagulability (memorize this!)
  • Risk factors: surgery, immobility, obesity, cancer, pregnancy, oral contraceptives
  • Unilateral leg swelling, warmth, redness, pain (Homans' sign is unreliable but still tested)
  • Pulmonary embolism is the most dangerous complication
Nursing Interventions
  • Prevention: early ambulation post-surgery, SCDs (sequential compression devices), anticoagulants
  • DO NOT massage the affected leg (can dislodge the clot)
  • Anticoagulation: heparin (acute), then warfarin or DOACs for maintenance
  • Elevate affected extremity; apply warm, moist compresses for comfort
  • Monitor for signs of PE: sudden dyspnea, chest pain, tachycardia
