DNA, Chromosomes & Gene Expression

Every cell in your body is carrying the same instruction manual, written in a four-letter alphabet, folded small enough to fit in a nucleus a few thousandths of a millimeter wide. This guide shows how that manual is built, packed, copied, read, and sometimes misprinted.

The whole story in one hospital

Picture your cell as a busy hospital. DNA is the master medical chart, locked in the Nurse's Station (the nucleus). It never leaves that room. When the floor needs a medication (a protein), the cell copies just the needed instructions onto a disposable sticky note (mRNA) and sends that note out to the floor (the ribosome), where the order is filled.

Why bother with a copy?The master chart is precious. If the original were sent onto the floor, every trip would risk damage to the only complete record. A disposable copy can be made, used, and thrown away, and the original stays safe. Cells figured out good document control long before hospitals did.

2. Nucleotides: the letters of the code

DNA is a polymer, and its monomer is the nucleotide. Every nucleotide has exactly three components:

  • a phosphate group
  • a pentose sugar (a 5-carbon sugar)
  • a nitrogenous base: ATGC

The phosphate and sugar are identical in every DNA nucleotide. Only the base changes.

Here is the astonishing partYou, a mushroom, and a blue whale all write your DNA with the same four bases. Nothing about the letters themselves is special to humans. Every difference between species, and between you and your siblings, comes from the sequence (order) of the nucleotides. Same alphabet, different sentences.

3. Base pairing & Chargaff's rules

The two strands of the double helix zip together down the middle with hydrogen bonds between complementary bases, and only two pairings are allowed:

A ↔ T   Apples in the Tree
G ↔ C   Cars in the Garage

Because every A has a T partner, %A = %T. Because every G has a C partner, %G = %C. That one idea solves every Chargaff math problem.

Worked example

A DNA sample is 22% adenine. What percent is cytosine?

  1. A = T, so T = 22%. A + T = 44%.
  2. What is left is G + C: 100% − 44% = 56%.
  3. G = C, so split it: 56% ÷ 2 = 28% cytosine.

Yes → RNA. Uracil never appears in DNA.

Module 4

5. Chromosomes: packing the chart

The DNA in one of your cells, stretched out, is far longer than the cell itself. The cell solves this the way you would store a very long thread: it winds it onto spools.

  • Chromosome: a complex of DNA and associated proteins carrying part or all of the cell's genetic information.
  • Histones: the protein spools that eukaryotic DNA wraps around.
  • Sister chromatids: two identical copies of one replicated chromosome, held together at the centromere.
  • Homologous chromosomes: a matching pair, one from mom and one from dad, with the same length, shape, and genes.
  • Autosomes: the non-sex chromosomes, the same in males and females.
Sisters vs. homologsSister chromatids are identical copies made during replication and stuck together at one centromere. Homologous chromosomes are two separate chromosomes, one from each parent; they carry the same genes but are not identical copies.

Counting chromosomes

Haploid1 set = 23

Diploid2 sets = 46

Most humans have 46 chromosomes in a diploid body cell. A diploid cell has two sets, so it holds 2 times the chromosome number of a haploid cell.

6. Karyotypes: reading the lineup

A karyotype is an organized image of an individual's complete set of chromosomes, lined up in numbered pairs. Two quick checks do most of the work:

Check 1: Count the total.
46 → typical count.
Not 46 → a chromosome is missing or extra. Find the pair that does not have exactly two.
Check 2: Look at pair #23.
XX → female.
XY → male.
⚕ Clinical connection: Down syndromeThree copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21) gives a total of 47 chromosomes and causes Down syndrome. A karyotype is one of the tests used to confirm the diagnosis, which is why the "count the total" check matters.

7. DNA replication: copying the chart before shift change

When: during the S (Synthesis) phase of the cell cycle, before the cell divides. Each daughter cell needs its own complete chart.

The model: replication is semiconservative. Each new double helix keeps one old (original) strand and gets one newly made strand.

Original helixold + old
→replicate
Copy 1old + new
Copy 2old + new
Why "semi" makes so much senseBase pairing means each strand already contains the information for its partner. Peel the two strands apart and each one is a perfect template: an A on the old strand demands a T on the new one, every time. The cell does not need a separate blueprint for the copy; the old strand is the blueprint.

The step order

1. Unwindenzymes separate the two strands
→
2. Primeprimers base-pair with the single strands

3. BuildDNA polymerase adds complementary nucleotides
  • Primer: a short, single strand of DNA or RNA that base-pairs with the template and creates an attachment point.
  • DNA polymerase: attaches to the primer and adds complementary nucleotides to build the new strand.
Troubleshooting card: "polymerase goes first"DNA polymerase is the most famous enzyme in the story, so it is tempting to put it first or second. It cannot start a new strand on bare template. No primer, no polymerase. Unwind → prime → build.
⚕ Clinical connection: why chemo causes hair lossMany chemotherapy drugs work by interfering with DNA replication, which hits rapidly dividing cells hardest. Cancer cells divide rapidly, but so do hair follicles, the lining of the GI tract, and bone marrow. That is why patients on these drugs commonly have hair loss, mouth sores, nausea, and low blood counts.

2. Step 1: Transcription (DNA → mRNA)

The enzyme: RNA polymerase builds the mRNA strand in eukaryotes. (Making RNA? RNA polymerase. Copying DNA? DNA polymerase.)

How to transcribe, every time

  1. Pair each template base with its RNA partner: A→U   T→A   G→C   C→G
  2. Flip the ends: a template read 3′ → 5′ gives mRNA written 5′ → 3′ (antiparallel).
  3. Final check: there is no T anywhere in the mRNA.

DNA template 3′ TGACAAGCT 5′

mRNA product 5′ ACUGUUCGA 3′

3. Step 2: RNA splicing (editing the rough draft)

Before eukaryotic mRNA leaves the nucleus, it gets edited like a movie reel. The non-coding parts are cut out and the coding parts are joined together.

  • Introns: non-coding sections spliced (cut) out before translation.
  • Exons: coding sections that stay in the final mature mRNA and code for the amino acid sequence.

EXons are EXpressed. INtrons are INtervening.

The name trap"Ex" sounds like "excised" (cut out), and that pulls students the wrong way. Exons are not excised. They are expressed: they leave the nucleus and become protein.

4. Step 3: Translation (mRNA → protein)

On the ribosome, the mRNA is read three bases at a time. Each three-base word is a codon, and each codon calls for one amino acid.

  • Codon: three nucleotides on the mRNA that specify an amino acid.
  • Anticodon: the complementary three nucleotides on the tRNA that match the codon.
  • tRNA (transfer RNA): the delivery truck. It carries one amino acid to the ribosome.

5. Mutations: typos in the master chart

Definition: a mutation is a permanent change in the DNA sequence of a chromosome. Permanent means it is not temporary or intermittent: it stays in the sequence unless it is repaired, and it can be copied and passed on.

Mutation typeWhat happensExample
Base-pair substitutionOne base is swapped for anotherSickle cell: mRNA codon GAG becomes GUG (A swapped for U)
InsertionOne or more nucleotides are addedAn extra base is pushed into the sequence
DeletionOne or more nucleotides are removedCystic fibrosis; a form of thalassemia (beta-globin gene)

Insertion adds; deletion deletes; substitution swaps.

UV light damage

Ultraviolet (UV) light causes nucleotide dimers (thymine dimers): neighboring bases on the same strand get stuck together, which puts a physical kink in the double helix.

Why location matters: exon vs. intron

Is the mutation inside an exon?
Yes → exons stay in the mature mRNA, so the change can alter the amino acid sequence of the protein.
In an intron → introns are spliced out before translation, so the change is less likely to reach the protein.
⚕ Clinical connection: one letter, one diseaseSickle cell anemia comes from a single base-pair substitution in the beta-globin gene. One swapped base changes one amino acid in hemoglobin, and red blood cells can deform into a sickle shape, block small vessels, and cause pain crises.
Cystic fibrosis is caused by a deletion. It produces thick, sticky mucus that affects the lungs and digestive system.
⚕ Clinical connection: sunscreen teachingUV-induced dimers are one reason sun exposure raises skin cancer risk. When you teach patients about sunscreen, protective clothing, and avoiding tanning beds, you are really teaching them to protect their DNA.

Review

Self-check: commit to an answer, then open it

Say your answer out loud or write it down before you click. Retrieval is what makes it stick.

1. A DNA sample is 26% adenine. Give the percentage of all four bases.
A = 26%, T = 26% (A = T). That is 52%, leaving 48% for G + C, so G = 24% and C = 24%. Check: 26 + 26 + 24 + 24 = 100.
2. Transcribe the DNA template strand 3′ TTACGG 5′.
5′ AAUGCC 3′. T→A, T→A, A→U, C→G, G→C, G→C, with the ends flipped. No T in the answer.
3. A sequence reads 5′ GCAUUA 3′. DNA or RNA? How do you know?
RNA, because it contains uracil (U). Uracil never appears in DNA.
4. A classmate says, "Watson and Crick proved DNA is the genetic material." Correct the statement.
Watson and Crick built the double helix model. Hershey and Chase used bacteriophages to show that DNA, not protein, is the genetic material injected into bacteria.
5. A karyotype shows 47 chromosomes, three copies of chromosome 21, and XY at pair 23. What is the biological sex, and what condition is shown?
Male (XY). Trisomy 21, Down syndrome (46 + 1 extra = 47).
6. A classmate puts DNA polymerase as the first step of replication. What is wrong?
Polymerase cannot start on bare template. The order is: enzymes unwind and separate the strands, primers base-pair with the single strands, and only then does DNA polymerase attach to the primer and add complementary nucleotides.
7. Explain "semiconservative" in one sentence, and say when replication happens.
Each new double helix contains one original strand and one newly made strand. It happens during S (synthesis) phase.
8. Two mutations are the same size: one in an intron, one in an exon. Which is more likely to change the protein, and why?
The exon mutation. Exons stay in the mature mRNA and code for the amino acid sequence, while introns are spliced out before translation.
9. Which molecule carries the codon and which carries the anticodon? What does the tRNA deliver?
The codon is on the mRNA; the anticodon is on the tRNA. The tRNA delivers one amino acid to the ribosome.
10. Using the hospital analogy, why does the cell send mRNA to the ribosome instead of the DNA itself?
DNA is the master chart and stays protected in the nucleus. mRNA is a disposable copy of just the needed instructions, so the original is never put at risk on "the floor" (the ribosome).
11. Name the damage UV light causes in DNA and explain why the word "permanent" matters for mutations.
Nucleotide (thymine) dimers, which kink the helix. A mutation is a permanent change in the sequence: unless it is repaired, it stays and can be copied during S phase and passed on to daughter cells.
12. Which enzyme makes mRNA, and which copies DNA?
RNA polymerase makes mRNA (transcription). DNA polymerase copies DNA (replication).
