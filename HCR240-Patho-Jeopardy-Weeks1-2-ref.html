Week 2 Pathophysiology Reference

Disease steps, clinical pearls, and nursing interventions

Type I: Immediate Hypersensitivity

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

First exposure to an allergen (pollen, food, drug) — the body says "I don't like you" and sensitizes

Step 2

B cells produce IgE antibodies specific to that allergen

Step 3

IgE antibodies bind to the surface of mast cells and basophils (now they're loaded and waiting)

Step 4

Re-exposure: allergen cross-links the IgE on mast cells → DEGRANULATION

Step 5

Massive release of histamine, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins

Step 6

Vasodilation, increased permeability, bronchoconstriction, itching, swelling — allergic response!

Clinical Pearls
  • IgE is the antibody class to remember for Type I
  • Mast cells are the star players — they hold and release histamine
  • Anaphylaxis is the most severe form: airway swelling + cardiovascular collapse
  • Examples: hay fever, asthma, food allergies, bee sting reactions
Nursing Interventions
  • Assess for airway compromise (stridor, wheezing, lip/tongue swelling)
  • Epinephrine IM (EpiPen) is the FIRST-LINE treatment for anaphylaxis
  • Antihistamines (diphenhydramine/Benadryl) for mild reactions
  • Teach patients to carry an EpiPen and wear a medical alert bracelet
  • Monitor for biphasic reaction (symptoms can return 4-8 hours later)

Type II: Cytotoxic Hypersensitivity

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Antibodies (IgG or IgM) are produced against antigens on cell surfaces

Step 2

Antibodies bind directly to the target cell's surface antigens

Step 3

Complement system activates → forms the Membrane Attack Complex (MAC)

Step 4

Target cells are lysed (destroyed) — think of it like popping a water balloon

Clinical Pearls
  • Classic example: ABO blood transfusion reactions (wrong blood type = cell destruction)
  • Erythroblastosis fetalis (Rh incompatibility) is a Type II reaction
  • "Cytotoxic" = "cell killing" — antibodies target cells for destruction
  • Also involved in drug-induced hemolytic anemia
Nursing Interventions
  • ALWAYS verify blood type and crossmatch before transfusions (two-nurse verification)
  • Monitor for transfusion reaction signs: fever, chills, flank pain, hemoglobinuria
  • If reaction occurs: STOP the transfusion immediately, keep the line open with NS
  • RhoGAM (Rh immunoglobulin) given to Rh-negative mothers at 28 weeks and within 72 hours postpartum

Type III: Immune Complex Hypersensitivity

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Antigen-antibody complexes (immune complexes) form in the bloodstream

Step 2

Normally the body clears these, but in excess they accumulate

Step 3

Immune complexes deposit in tissues (joints, kidneys, blood vessels, skin)

Step 4

Complement activates at deposition sites → attracts neutrophils

Step 5

Neutrophils release lysosomal enzymes → tissue damage and inflammation

Clinical Pearls
  • Think of it as "trash piles" clogging up your organs
  • SLE (Lupus) is the classic Type III disease
  • ANA (antinuclear antibody) and anti-dsDNA are key lab markers for SLE
  • Can affect virtually ANY organ system
Nursing Interventions
  • Monitor renal function (BUN, creatinine) — kidney damage is a major complication
  • Teach sun avoidance (UV light triggers flares in lupus)
  • Administer immunosuppressants and corticosteroids as ordered
  • Assess for butterfly rash, joint pain, fatigue during flares

Type IV: Delayed (Cell-Mediated) Hypersensitivity

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Antigen is processed by antigen-presenting cells (macrophages, dendritic cells)

Step 2

Sensitized T-cells recognize the antigen (takes 24-72 hours — that's why it's "delayed")

Step 3

T-cells release cytokines → recruit macrophages and other inflammatory cells

Step 4

Direct cell-mediated cytotoxicity OR delayed inflammatory response at the site

Clinical Pearls
  • NO antibodies involved — this is purely T-cell mediated
  • Takes 24-72 hours to develop (hence "delayed")
  • Classic example: contact dermatitis (poison ivy), TB skin test (PPD), organ transplant rejection
  • Only hypersensitivity type that is NOT antibody-mediated
Nursing Interventions
  • Read TB skin test at 48-72 hours (measure induration, not redness)
  • Teach patients to avoid known contact allergens
  • Topical corticosteroids for contact dermatitis
  • For transplant patients: administer immunosuppressants, monitor for rejection signs

Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Immune system loses tolerance to self-antigens (stops recognizing "self" as friendly)

Step 2

Autoantibodies (ANA, anti-dsDNA) are produced against the body's own nuclear material

Step 3

Antigen-antibody immune complexes form in the blood (Type III hypersensitivity)

Step 4

Complexes deposit in tissues: kidneys, joints, skin, heart, lungs, brain

Step 5

Complement activation → inflammation and tissue damage at every deposition site

Step 6

Chronic relapsing-remitting course with multi-organ involvement

Clinical Pearls
  • The "Great Imitator" — mimics many other diseases
  • Butterfly (malar) rash across nose and cheeks is the hallmark
  • More common in women of childbearing age, especially African American women
  • Sunlight exposure can trigger flares
  • Lupus nephritis is the most serious complication
Nursing Interventions
  • Teach sun protection (SPF 30+, protective clothing, avoid peak sun hours)
  • Monitor for signs of renal involvement (proteinuria, edema, elevated creatinine)
  • Administer medications as ordered: NSAIDs, hydroxychloroquine, corticosteroids
  • Encourage rest during flares, balanced activity during remission
  • Emotional support — chronic disease with unpredictable course

Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Autoimmune trigger (genetic predisposition + environmental factor)

Step 2

T-cells activate against synovial tissue in the joints

Step 3

Inflammatory cytokines recruit more immune cells to the synovium

Step 4

Synovial membrane becomes inflamed and thickened (pannus formation)

Step 5

Pannus erodes cartilage and bone → joint destruction and deformity

Clinical Pearls
  • Bilateral and symmetrical joint involvement (both hands, both knees)
  • Morning stiffness lasting >1 hour is characteristic
  • Rheumatoid factor (RF) and anti-CCP antibodies are key labs
  • Swan neck and boutonniere deformities of the fingers in advanced disease
Nursing Interventions
  • DMARDs (methotrexate) should be started early to slow joint destruction
  • Apply heat for stiffness, cold for acute inflammation
  • Encourage joint-protection techniques and adaptive devices
  • Monitor for DMARD side effects (liver toxicity, immunosuppression)
