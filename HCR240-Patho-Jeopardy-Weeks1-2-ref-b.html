Selective IgA Deficiency

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Genetic defect prevents B cells from maturing into IgA-producing plasma cells

Step 2

IgA levels are absent or severely reduced

Step 3

Mucosal surfaces (respiratory, GI, GU tracts) lose their first line of immune defense

Step 4

Increased susceptibility to infections at mucosal surfaces

Clinical Pearls
  • Most common primary immunodeficiency worldwide
  • Many patients are actually asymptomatic!
  • When symptomatic: recurrent sinopulmonary and GI infections in young children
  • Can cause anaphylaxis if given blood products containing IgA
Nursing Interventions
  • Use washed or IgA-deficient blood products to prevent anaphylactic transfusion reactions
  • Teach infection prevention: hand hygiene, avoid sick contacts
  • Prompt treatment of infections with antibiotics
  • No IVIG replacement (the issue is IgA, not IgG)

DiGeorge Syndrome

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Deletion on chromosome 22 (22q11.2) during fetal development

Step 2

Third and fourth pharyngeal pouches fail to develop properly

Step 3

Thymus is absent or underdeveloped → T-cells can't mature

Step 4

Parathyroid glands also missing → hypocalcemia

Step 5

Congenital heart defects often present (conotruncal abnormalities)

Clinical Pearls
  • Remember: "CATCH-22" — Cardiac, Abnormal facies, Thymic aplasia, Cleft palate, Hypocalcemia, chromosome 22
  • T-cell deficiency (not B-cell) — cell-mediated immunity is impaired
  • Tetany and seizures from hypocalcemia due to missing parathyroids
Nursing Interventions
  • Monitor calcium levels; administer calcium and vitamin D supplements
  • Assess for signs of hypocalcemia (Chvostek's sign, Trousseau's sign, tetany)
  • Infection precautions due to T-cell deficiency
  • Avoid live vaccines (impaired T-cell immunity)
  • Coordinate with cardiology for heart defect management

HIV / AIDS

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

HIV (a retrovirus) enters the body through blood, sexual contact, or vertical transmission

Step 2

Virus binds to CD4+ T helper cells using the CD4 receptor and co-receptors

Step 3

Reverse transcriptase converts viral RNA to DNA, which integrates into the host genome

Step 4

Virus replicates and destroys CD4+ cells over time

Step 5

Progressive decline in CD4+ count → immune system collapses

Step 6

AIDS diagnosed when CD4+ count <200 cells/μL or opportunistic infection develops

Clinical Pearls
  • HIV attacks the CD4+ T helper cells — the "quarterbacks" of the immune system
  • Normal CD4+ count: 500-1500 cells/μL
  • Opportunistic infections (PCP pneumonia, Kaposi sarcoma, candidiasis) signal AIDS
  • Viral load measures disease activity; CD4+ count measures immune status
Nursing Interventions
  • Antiretroviral therapy (ART) — emphasize medication adherence (missing doses = resistance)
  • Monitor CD4+ count and viral load regularly
  • Standard precautions; educate on transmission prevention
  • Screen for opportunistic infections; prophylaxis when CD4+ drops
  • Provide emotional support and connect with resources

Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Malignant transformation of lymphoid precursor cells in the bone marrow

Step 2

Immature lymphoblasts proliferate uncontrollably

Step 3

Lymphoblasts crowd out normal bone marrow cells

Step 4

Normal RBC, WBC, and platelet production is suppressed

Step 5

Anemia (low RBCs), infections (low functional WBCs), bleeding (low platelets)

Clinical Pearls
  • Most common childhood cancer — peak age 2-5 years
  • Cure rate exceeds 85% with treatment
  • Symptoms: fatigue, fever, frequent infections, bruising/bleeding, bone pain
  • Diagnosed by bone marrow biopsy showing >20% blasts
Nursing Interventions
  • Infection prevention is priority #1 (neutropenic precautions)
  • Monitor for bleeding (petechiae, bruising, blood in stool/urine)
  • Manage chemotherapy side effects (nausea, mucositis, fatigue)
  • Emotional support for child AND family

Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL)

▼
Pathophysiology Steps
Step 1

Malignant transformation of mature-appearing but dysfunctional B lymphocytes

Step 2

B cells fail to differentiate into fully functional plasma cells

Step 3

Immature B cells accumulate slowly in blood, bone marrow, and lymph nodes

Step 4

Lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly develop from B cell accumulation

Step 5

Immune function gradually declines → increased infection risk

Clinical Pearls
  • Most common leukemia in ADULTS, especially the elderly
  • Slow-progressing — many patients are asymptomatic at diagnosis (found on routine labs)
  • Hallmark: extremely high lymphocyte count on CBC
  • B cells are the problem (not T cells)
Nursing Interventions
  • "Watch and wait" may be the initial approach for early-stage disease
  • Monitor CBC trends and lymph node enlargement
  • Infection prevention education
  • Assess for fatigue and activity intolerance
