The Town of pH

On lab day you will mix six beakers and watch a town hold its nerve - or fail to. Four of them are towns with two brothers on duty. Two of them are towns with nobody home.

This is the same town you already know: outlaws (H⁺), deputies, and a hat that is the proton. The only difference is that today the brothers have real names - acetic acid and acetate, ammonium and ammonia - and the hats being passed around are the ones printed in your Table 2.

1

The Town That Acid Never Leaves

The solution - under siege the moment it exists

Beakers 1 and 2 are towns with nobody home. You put distilled water in them and nothing else - no brothers, no hats, no law. Every drop of 0.1 M HCl rides straight in and sits there, because there is no one to take its hat.

That is why the ΔpH for beakers 1 and 2 will be enormous, and it is the number everything else in this lab gets measured against. When you fill in Table 2, those two rows are your baseline - what a town does with no buffer.

Beakers 3 through 6 are towns with two brothers standing on the porch. Watch what changes.

2

Two Brothers, One Hat

The two requirements - weak, and one proton apart

Today the brothers wear two different uniforms, but the drawing is unchanged - same build, same boots, same stance. Sheriff CH₃COOH holds the hat; Deputy CH₃COO⁻ is the same brother with the hat off. One hat. That hat is the proton, and it is the entire difference between them.

And the Sheriff is weak - he holds the hat loose, giving almost none of it away. Beaker 3 is that town: 5 mL of 0.1 M acetic acid plus 5 mL of 0.1 M sodium acetate. Same brother, hat on and hat off, standing in one beaker. That is what "buffer" means on your bench.

Why "weak" is doing your homework for you

A strong acid hands out its hats immediately and completely - nothing is left over to catch the next outlaw. A weak acid keeps nearly all of them, so a large reserve is always on hand. That reserve is the buffer. Before lab, work it out for beaker 3: acetic acid has Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵, so at 0.1 M only about 1.3% of its molecules have given up a hat - roughly 98.7% are still loaded and waiting.

Ka = [H⁺][CH₃COO⁻] / [CH₃COOH] = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ let [H⁺] = [CH₃COO⁻] = x, and [CH₃COOH] ≈ 0.1 x² / 0.1 = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ → x² = 1.8 × 10⁻⁶ x = 1.34 × 10⁻³ M % ionized = (1.34 × 10⁻³ / 0.1) × 100 ≈ 1.3% check: pH = −log(1.34 × 10⁻³) ≈ 2.87 for the acid alone

Notice what that means: the acid is weak not because it is dilute, but because of that tiny Ka. Diluting a strong acid does not make it weak. Weak is about how tightly the molecule holds the hat.

One caveat about your readings: at 0.1 M the acid is 1.3% ionized and reads about 2.87. If your lab manual works this from 1.0 M instead, the same calculation gives x = 4.2 × 10⁻³ M and pH ≈ 2.37. Either way it is nowhere near the 1.07 a 0.1 M strong acid gives you.

3

An Outlaw Rides In - Beakers 1, 3, 5

Five drops of 0.1 M HCl, and the bare brother does the work

The outlaw comes over the ridge. Deputy CH₃COO⁻ does not shoot him - he takes his hat. The outlaw is disarmed, stays in town, and the Deputy becomes the Sheriff.

Read the ledger: acetate falls, acetic acid rises, and the loose H⁺ count barely moves. The proton did not vanish; it changed hands. That is your small ΔpH in beaker 3 - and beaker 1, with nobody to take a hat, is your enormous one.

Beaker 3 - the acetate buffer meets HCl  ·  this is discussion Q4
CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq) → CH₃COOH(aq)

The added H⁺ is consumed by the conjugate base to make more weak acid. Free H⁺ stays low, so the pH barely falls.

Beaker 5 - the ammonia buffer, hats counted the other way  ·  this is discussion Q6
NH₃(aq) + H⁺(aq) → NH₄⁺(aq)

Look at what just happened to the roles. In beaker 3 the bare brother took the hat. In beaker 5 the one who takes the hat is NH₃, and the brother who wears it is NH₄⁺. The ammonia buffer is the same story with the hats counted the other way, because its weak acid (NH₄⁺, pKa = 9.25) holds the hat far more tightly than acetic acid does.

4

The Man Who Comes for Hats - Beakers 2, 4, 6

Five drops of 0.1 M NaOH, and the hatted brother does the work

Ollie is not here to cause trouble. He is here collecting. Sheriff CH₃COOH tips his hat, Ollie rides out satisfied and wearing it as H₂O, and the Sheriff becomes the Deputy. Ledger: acetic acid falls, acetate rises, free OH⁻ gone, ΔpH small.

And here is the line most students lose this lab on: the proton was not destroyed. It left town on Ollie's head. Nothing was cancelled. The hat is the same hat - it belongs to someone else now.

Beaker 4 - the acetate buffer meets NaOH  ·  this is discussion Q3
CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)

OH⁻ takes the proton from the weak acid. The acid is used up to make more conjugate base, and the hydroxide leaves as water.

Beaker 6 - the ammonia buffer meets NaOH  ·  this is discussion Q5
NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l)

Same shape as beaker 4, hats counted the other way. Here the hatted brother (NH₄⁺) is the one who gives the hat up. Four beakers, four reactions, one story - that is the whole trick of this lab in a single sentence.

5

The Balance Beam Out Back

Le Chatelier's principle - already running before you added a drop

Behind the saloon the brothers keep a standing swap going. Hat on. Hat off. It never stops, and it was running before your dropper ever touched the beaker. So when the outlaw shoves, the beam leans - and then swings back, because a swap already in motion absorbs a shove by rebalancing itself.

That is Le Chatelier's principle. Beaker 3 was a shove from the left; beaker 4 was a shove from the right. Same beam. Both directions give you a small ΔpH - and beakers 1 and 2, with no beam at all, give you the big numbers that prove it.

Reading your own ΔpH like a chemist

ΔpH = Final pH − Initial pH
Distilled water (1, 2): ΔpH large - no conjugate base or acid is present to absorb the added ion, so it stays free.
Acetate buffer (3, 4): ΔpH small - the acetate/acetic acid pair absorbs both H⁺ and OH⁻.
Ammonia buffer (5, 6): ΔpH small - the NH₃/NH₄⁺ pair does the same job on the basic side.

Then ask the better question: why does beaker 3 sit near pH 4.7 while beaker 5 sits near pH 9.3, when both resist change equally well? Because a buffer does not fix a pH - it defends the pH it was built around, its own pKa. Same deputies, different neighborhoods.

6

When the Jail Fills Up

Buffer capacity - and the four things your ΔpH column is telling you

Five drops fits. Five hundred might not. Buffer capacity is how much abuse a town can take before the cells fill, and it is finite - the brothers are two, not infinite. Flood beaker 4 with enough NaOH and you will convert nearly every acetic acid molecule into acetate. At that point the town has no Sheriff left, and Ollie's next raid has nothing to push against.

Your body has a depot - lungs blow off CO₂ within seconds, kidneys reclaim HCO₃⁻ over hours to days. Your beaker has no depot at all. That is why your blood survives a lemon and your beaker does not.

Four things your ΔpH column is quietly telling you

1 · Beakers 1 and 2 are the control. A large ΔpH means no buffer was present. Every "small" number in your table only means something compared with these.
2 · The pair works from both sides. A small ΔpH on the HCl row and the NaOH row is the signature of a real buffer.
3 · Capacity is a stock, not a speed. A buffer resists a slow drip of acid exactly as well as a sudden drop - until the stock runs out.
4 · Two buffers, same job, different pH. Acetate defends ~4.7; ammonia defends ~9.3. Resistance is not a fixed pH - it is a range around a pKa.

Your buffer equations for the Conclusions section
CH₃COOH(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)
NH₄⁺(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₃(aq) + H₃O⁺(aq)

The ⇌ is the balance beam, drawn as an arrow. It is not decoration - it is the whole mechanism. Strong acids and bases get a one-way arrow (→); a weak acid in water gets the double arrow (⇌). If you write a single arrow for a buffer, you have drawn a town with no swap.

7

The Rumor at the Back of the Saloon

The myth this lab disproves twice - with your own beakers

Students walk in believing the acid half must run below pH 7 and the base half above 7. Nobody posted that rule. The only requirements are weak and conjugate - hats held loose, one hat apart.

Then look at your own bench. Beaker 3 defends a pH near 4.7, well below 7. Beaker 5 defends a pH near 9.3, well above 7. Both of them resist pH change beautifully. A buffer does not care which side of 7 it lives on. It only needs its pair.

Where a conjugate base gets its basic side  ·  this is discussion Q2
CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ CH₃COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq)

Sodium acetate does not contain OH⁻. Neither does sodium carbonate. What they contain is a conjugate base - and a conjugate base takes a proton from water, leaving OH⁻ behind. That is why the solution is basic even though you never added a base.

Now test yourself on the strength question. Put a proton on that acetate ion. What do you get? Acetic acid - a weak acid. So acetate is a weak base: strong enough to raise the pH, too weak to be called strong. The strong/weak of a conjugate pair are always opposite - that is the rule hiding inside the equation.

Before you write the Conclusions paragraph

Compare ΔpH across each row, not just the pH values. Beakers 1, 3 and 5 all got the same five drops of HCl - and only two of them barely moved. Then compare beaker 3 to beaker 5: both resisted, at very different pH. That comparison is your conclusion. Do not just report the numbers - say what the numbers were competing with.

ΔpH = Final pH − Initial pH  ·  a negative ΔpH means acid was added; a positive ΔpH means base was added. Watch the signs in Table 2 and make sure they match your row.

THE 5 THINGS YOU MUST KNOW

  1. Two requirements, no more: a weak acid and its conjugate base - CH₃COOH / CH₃COO⁻ or NH₄⁺ / NH₃.
  2. Acid in → the bare brother takes the hat (A⁻ + H⁺ → HA). Base in → the hatted brother gives it up (HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O).
  3. Nothing is destroyed. The proton changes hands or leaves as water - it never vanishes.
  4. Weak acids use ⇌, strong ones use →, and the strong/weak of a conjugate pair are always opposite.
  5. No pH-7 rule. Acetate defends ~4.7, ammonia defends ~9.3, and your ΔpH proves both resist.

THE COMMON MISTAKE

Three ways students lose this lab. One: believing the buffer cancels acid into nothing - it shifts an equilibrium, and the proton always goes somewhere. Two: assuming a buffer forces the pH to 7. It does not; it defends its own pKa, which is why beaker 3 sits at 4.7 and beaker 5 at 9.3. Three: writing → for a weak acid. A one-way arrow says the hat never comes back - and if the hat never comes back, there is no buffer, only a one-time cleanup.

MEMORY HOOK

"The hat is the proton. Weak hands hold it loose. Brothers share it."
Beakers 1 and 2 have no brothers - that is your big ΔpH. Beakers 3-6 have two each - that is your small ΔpH. HCl rides in → the bare twin takes a hat. NaOH rides in → the hatted twin tips his. And ⇌ means the hat comes back.

IF SOMEONE ASKS "WHAT IS A BUFFER?"

Two conjugate brothers - the same twin, one proton apart - sitting in the same beaker. One holds the proton loosely enough to give it up when base arrives, and one is bare enough to catch it when acid arrives. So whichever one rides in, the pH barely flinches - until the brothers run out.

Cast key - outlaw = H⁺ · Sheriff = the weak acid (HA) · Deputy = the conjugate base (A⁻) · Ollie = OH⁻ · the hat = the proton · the balance beam = HA ⇌ A⁻ + H⁺ · the train = excretion (lungs, kidneys). On your bench today: beakers 3-4 run acetate (CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻, pKa 4.74) and beakers 5-6 run ammonia (NH₄⁺/NH₃, pKa 9.25); beakers 1-2 are water with no brothers at all.
