A buffer story in ten chapters, told the old way

The Town of pH

Every buffer is a weak acid standing next to its conjugate base, and the two of them differ by exactly one proton. In this town, the proton wears a hat. Same outlaws, same deputies, but this telling has a head count, a home turf, a posse, a railroad, and a rumor that needs killing.

Pull up a chair. You've got the buffer exam coming, and the textbook keeps saying "conjugate" like you're supposed to already know what it means. I know. I sat right where you're sitting. So let me tell it to you the way it finally made sense to me, as a story about a little town out West.

But here's the deal I'm making with you. Every picture in this story is a hook, and on every hook I've hung a real piece of chemistry. You'll find it in the boxes marked What the chemists write down. Don't you skip those. The story is the trail. The chemistry is where the trail takes you. Remember one, and you'll pull the other right up along with it.

1

The Town

The solution, where acid shows up whether you asked for it or not

Outlaws. Hydrogen ions, H⁺. They ride into town all day and all night, and nobody ever sent them an invitation. Your cells make acid just by staying alive. Every bite of food you burn, every flight of stairs, every quiet hour your metabolism keeps ticking, more of them show up at the edge of town.

Here's one that surprises people. Much of the CO₂ your cells make gets turned into carbonic acid in your blood, with an enzyme called carbonic anhydrase doing the work, and it rides around that way until your lungs take it back out. So the town isn't really a place. It's the solution. Your beaker, or your blood. And without law standing ready at every hour, it falls apart.

What the chemists write down

pH = −log [H⁺]

pH is the town's lawlessness meter, read upside down. More outlaws, lower number. And the scale is logarithmic, so every step of 1 means ten times as many outlaws.

Your blood sits at pH 7.40, which works out to about 40 nanomoles of H⁺ per liter. At pH 7.10 it's about 80. That 0.3 drop looks like nothing on paper. In a patient, it means the outlaws have doubled.

2

The Brothers

Two requirements, two twins. Weak, and one proton apart.

The buffer is run by twins. Sheriff H.A. wears the hat. Deputy A⁻ is the very same brother with the hat off. The hat is the proton. One hat is the whole difference between them, and that's all "conjugate" means: two species that differ by exactly one proton.

Now, about that word weak. People get this one backwards, so let me be careful with you. A weak acid isn't sloppy with his hat. He keeps it on his head most of the time. But it isn't glued on. He'll hand it over when somebody needs it, and his brother will take one back whenever there's one to be had. That back and forth is the whole trick.

Compare a strong acid, like hydrochloric acid. HCl doesn't wear his hat. He throws it in the dirt the second he rides into town and never looks at it again. His leftover twin, chloride (Cl⁻), will not pick a hat up. Not ever. So HCl and Cl⁻ can't run a buffer, because there's no deputy willing to do the job. That's why the acid has to be weak. It's the only way both brothers stay on the force.

What the chemists write down

HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻    Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA]    pKa = −log Ka

Ka measures how readily the Sheriff gives up his hat. A small Ka means he mostly keeps it, and that's exactly what "weak" means. pKa flips it into a friendlier number: the smaller the Ka, the bigger the pKa.

Acetic acid: Ka ≈ 1.75 × 10⁻⁵, so pKa ≈ 4.76. The carbonic acid / bicarbonate pair in plasma behaves as if its pKa were 6.1 (that number counts dissolved CO₂ on the Sheriff's side of the ledger). Hydrochloric acid gives its hat up so completely that it never comes back, which is exactly why it can't buffer.

Real twin pairs, one hat apart: CH₃COOH / CH₃COO⁻ · H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻ · H₂PO₄⁻ / HPO₄²⁻. Count the H's. Always one apart.

3

An Outlaw Rides In

Add acid, and the base half of the buffer absorbs it

An outlaw rides in. You just added acid. Now watch what the Deputy does, because it's not what you'd expect. He doesn't shoot the man. He takes his hat. That's all. And once the Deputy has a hat on his head, guess who he is? He's the Sheriff now.

Look at the ledger. One outlaw came in. One deputy got used up. One new sheriff appeared. The loose H⁺ count barely moves, and that's why pH holds. The proton didn't vanish. It didn't get destroyed. It just changed hands.

What the chemists write down

A⁻ + H⁺ → HA

This runs nearly to completion. One for one: one outlaw in, one deputy spent, one sheriff made. That strict bookkeeping is the first step of every buffer calculation you'll ever do, so get comfortable with it now.

In blood: HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O. Look at that last step. The new Sheriff turns into CO₂, and CO₂ can leave town. Keep that in your pocket until Chapter 9.

4

The Hat Thief

Add base, and the acid half of the buffer absorbs it

Now base rides in. This here is Ollie the Hat Thief, hydroxide (OH⁻), and Ollie is death on hats. The Sheriff hands his over without a fight. Ollie rides out of town satisfied, wearing the hat, as water (H₂O), and the Sheriff is left bare-headed. He's the Deputy now.

Here's the step most students miss, so stay with me. The proton is not destroyed. It leaves town on Ollie's head. H⁺ and OH⁻ together make water. That's neutralization, plain and simple. The proton went somewhere. It did not just disappear.

What the chemists write down

HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O

One for one again: one thief in, one sheriff spent, one deputy made. Chapters 3 and 4 are mirror images. Acid uses up deputies. Base uses up sheriffs. That's why a buffer needs both brothers on duty.

In blood: H₂CO₃ + OH⁻ → HCO₃⁻ + H₂O.

5

The Head Count

Henderson-Hasselbalch, or how far the pH actually moves

An old sheriff told me something once that I never forgot. "The mood of this town," he said, "isn't set by how many lawmen we've got. It's set by the head count. Bareheads to hats." He was right, and that might be the most important sentence in this whole story.

Say the town has 100 deputies and 100 sheriffs. Ten outlaws ride in. Ten deputies take ten hats. Now you've got 90 bareheads and 110 hats. The head count went from 100 to 100, to 90 to 110. That's a change, sure. But look how small it is next to the size of the posse. That's why the pH barely moves.

And Le Chatelier? He's still on the job. Push that swap one way, and it settles back into balance. Here's how to keep the two straight: Le Chatelier tells you which way the town leans. The head count tells you how far.

What the chemists write down

pH = pKa + log ( [A⁻] / [HA] )

Bareheads on top, hats on the bottom. This is the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, and it's nothing more than the head count written in math.

Worked example. One liter of acetate buffer, 0.100 M of each twin, pKa 4.76. At the start: pH = 4.76 + log(0.100 / 0.100) = 4.76 + 0 = 4.76. Add 0.010 mol of HCl. Deputies drop to 0.090, sheriffs rise to 0.110. New pH = 4.76 + log(0.090 / 0.110) = 4.76 − 0.09 = 4.67.

Now pour that same 0.010 mol of HCl into a liter of plain water with no buffer at all. The pH falls from 7.00 to 2.00. Five whole units. That's 100,000 times more outlaws. Same acid. One town had a posse, and the other didn't.

6

The Saloon Rumor

No, a buffer does not hold things at pH 7

Every saloon has a fella at the end of the bar who knows everything and is wrong about half of it. His favorite: "A buffer keeps things neutral. pH 7. That's the whole point of a buffer." I hear some version of that every single semester.

It's dead wrong. A buffer doesn't drag the town to 7. It holds the town wherever its own twins set it. An acetate posse holds near 4.76. The phosphate posse inside your cells holds near 7.2. Your blood holds at 7.40. A buffer's job is to resist change. It has nothing to do with neutral.

THE PROOF THAT KILLS THE RUMOR

Look at the head count equation again: pH = pKa + log(ratio). There's no 7 in it anywhere. The pH a buffer holds comes from exactly two things: how readily that Sheriff gives up his hat (pKa) and the head count (the ratio). An acetate buffer at a 1:1 ratio sits at pH 4.76, which is good and acidic, and it buffers beautifully right there. The rumor dies at the saloon door.

So here's the real law, the one nailed to the jailhouse wall: weak, conjugate, and working near your own pKa. That last part brings us to the next chapter.

7

Home Turf

Every buffer works best within one pH unit of its own pKa

Every sheriff has home turf. Inside it, he's hard to beat. Wander too far outside it, and he's a stranger in a strange town.

When the head count is even, as many bareheads as hats, the town is ready for trouble from either direction. That's the sweet spot, and it sits right at pH = pKa. Go one unit either way and the head count is ten to one. Past that, one brother has nearly disappeared, and the town can only defend one side. Too few deputies, and the outlaws take the town. Too few sheriffs, and Ollie cleans you out.

Now look at that ruler, and look hard at your blood. Bicarbonate's pKa is 6.1. Your blood runs at 7.40. That's 1.3 units away, outside the home turf. By the book, that ought to be a so-so buffer. Keep that in your pocket too. It's the best twist in the whole story, and Chapter 9 pays it off.

What the chemists write down

[A⁻] = [HA]  →  log(1) = 0  →  pH = pKa

Useful range: pKa ± 1, meaning a head count anywhere between 10:1 and 1:10.

To build a buffer for a target pH, pick a pair whose pKa sits close to that target. That's how chemists choose. Phosphate (pKa 7.2) is a favorite for work near neutral for exactly this reason.

Blood: the ratio of HCO₃⁻ to dissolved CO₂ is about 20:1. log 20 = 1.30, and 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40.

8

The Size of the Posse

Buffer capacity, and what happens when it runs out

Two towns. Same head count, same pH. One has ten lawmen. The other has ten thousand. Which one would you rather live in when a gang of fifty rides in?

That's buffer capacity. The head count sets the pH. The size of the posse sets how much trouble the town can take before it falls. Once the deputies are all spent, the next outlaw walks right in, and the pH drops like a stone.

And here's the hard truth about a beaker. It has no railroad. No backup is coming. Whatever posse you mixed in is all you'll ever have. Run through it, and that's the end of the town.

What the chemists write down

capacity grows with [HA] + [A⁻], and peaks when the ratio is near 1:1

Same pH, different capacity. A 0.010 M / 0.010 M acetate buffer and a 1.0 M / 1.0 M acetate buffer both sit at pH 4.76. Add 0.010 mol of HCl to a liter of each.

The big posse barely notices: 0.99 / 1.01 gives pH 4.75. The small posse loses every last deputy. With nothing but sheriffs left, the pH falls to about 3.2, and the very next drop of acid meets no resistance at all.

9

The Railroad

Why your blood is no beaker: the open system

Now here is where your body does something no glassware ever could. Pay attention to this one, because I've watched this exact idea separate the students who understand blood gases from the ones who memorize and pray.

The bicarbonate town has a railroad, and it runs every minute of every day. Not just when the jail fills up. All the time. The lungs control how many Sheriffs are in town. Remember Chapter 3? Every time a deputy takes an outlaw's hat, the new Sheriff turns into CO₂, and CO₂ can board the train. Every breath you let out carries Sheriffs out of town. Breathe faster, more of them leave. Breathe slower, they pile up.

The kidneys run the other line. They control the deputies, holding on to HCO₃⁻ or letting it go, and shipping H⁺ out in the urine. The lungs work in minutes. The kidneys take days, but they're thorough.

So here's the twist I promised you. In a closed beaker, a 20 to 1 head count sitting 1.3 units off home turf would make a so-so buffer. But this town never lets its Sheriffs pile up. Every outlaw that gets disarmed turns into CO₂ and gets put on a train. That's what makes bicarbonate the master buffer of your blood. Not the chemistry alone. The chemistry plus the railroad.

And notice who the town hired. Twenty deputies for every Sheriff. That's no accident. Your body makes acid all day long, so it hired for the trouble it actually gets.

What the chemists write down

CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻

The first step is sped up enormously by carbonic anhydrase, mostly inside red blood cells.

pH = 6.1 + log ( [HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × PaCO₂) )

The same head count equation, with the Sheriff's side written as dissolved CO₂. The 0.03 converts CO₂ pressure in mmHg into mmol/L.

Normal: HCO₃⁻ 24 mmol/L, PaCO₂ 40 mmHg. 0.03 × 40 = 1.2. 24 ÷ 1.2 = 20. log 20 = 1.30. pH = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40. Every blood gas you will ever read lives inside this one line.

Other posses help too: hemoglobin inside red cells, plasma proteins, and phosphate inside cells and in urine. Bicarbonate gets top billing because it's the only one with a railroad.

10

When the Railroad Breaks

Acidosis, alkalosis, and the town that fixes itself

I'll leave you with the part that matters at the bedside. Let me tell you about two towns. Both are sitting at pH 7.10. Both are in trouble. And they're in completely different kinds of trouble.

In the first town, something has been killing off the deputies. Maybe it's diabetic ketoacidosis pouring acid in. Maybe it's a gut losing bicarbonate. HCO₃⁻ has fallen from 24 to 12. The railroad is running just fine. That's a metabolic problem.

In the second town, the deputies are all present and accounted for, 24 strong. But the train has broken down. The lungs aren't moving air, and Sheriffs are piling up. PaCO₂ has climbed from 40 to 80. That's a respiratory problem.

Same pH. Different crime. Different fix. And here's the part that makes me love this town: when one line breaks, the other line picks up the slack. That's compensation.

TOWN ONE · THE DEPUTIES ARE GONE

6.1 + log(12 ÷ 1.2)
= 6.1 + log 10 = 7.10
Metabolic acidosis. The lungs answer by blowing off CO₂.

TOWN TWO · THE TRAIN BROKE DOWN

6.1 + log(24 ÷ 2.4)
= 6.1 + log 10 = 7.10
Respiratory acidosis. The kidneys answer by keeping HCO₃⁻.

These two towns are simplified to make the point. In a real patient, compensation starts right away, and a sudden rise in CO₂ nudges HCO₃⁻ up a little on its own.

What the clinicians write down

ROME: Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal

In a respiratory problem, pH and PaCO₂ move in opposite directions. In a metabolic problem, pH and HCO₃⁻ move in the same direction.

Normal ranges: pH 7.35 to 7.45 · PaCO₂ 35 to 45 mmHg · HCO₃⁻ 22 to 26 mmol/L.

Story check: Is it the train (lungs, CO₂) or the deputies (kidneys, HCO₃⁻)? Find what's abnormal, note which way it moved, and the town will tell you which line broke.

Problems at the Saloon Table

Try each one before you open it. The story will get you started; the math will finish it.

1. A liter of buffer has 0.50 M acetic acid and 0.50 M acetate (pKa 4.76). What's the pH?

Story: equal head count, so the town sits right at the Sheriff's pKa.
Math: pH = 4.76 + log(0.50 / 0.50) = 4.76 + 0 = 4.76.

2. Add 0.050 mol of HCl to that liter. What's the new pH? Then compare it to the same HCl in a liter of pure water.

Story: 0.050 mol of outlaws take 0.050 mol of hats. Deputies fall to 0.45, sheriffs rise to 0.55.
Math: pH = 4.76 + log(0.45 / 0.55) = 4.76 − 0.09 = 4.67. In pure water, [H⁺] = 0.050 M, so pH = 1.30. The posse saved more than three whole pH units.

3. You need to hold a solution at pH 7.0. Acetate (pKa 4.76) or phosphate (pKa 7.2)? Why?

Story: phosphate is on home turf. Acetate is more than two units away.
Math: at pH 7.0, acetate's head count would be about 170 deputies for every sheriff (102.24). Almost no sheriffs means almost no defense against base. Pick phosphate.

4. Blood gas: HCO₃⁻ 12 mmol/L, PaCO₂ 40 mmHg. What's the pH, and which line broke?

Math: pH = 6.1 + log(12 ÷ 1.2) = 6.1 + 1 = 7.10.
Story: the deputies are half gone and the train is normal. Metabolic acidosis. ROME check: pH down, HCO₃⁻ down, same direction, metabolic. Expect the lungs to start blowing off CO₂.

5. A patient in a panic attack: PaCO₂ 20 mmHg, HCO₃⁻ 24 mmol/L. What's the pH, and what's happening in town?

Math: 0.03 × 20 = 0.6. pH = 6.1 + log(24 ÷ 0.6) = 6.1 + log 40 = 6.1 + 1.60 = 7.70 (simplified, before any compensation).
Story: the train is running too fast and the Sheriffs are leaving in droves. Respiratory alkalosis. ROME check: pH up, PaCO₂ down, opposite directions, respiratory.

THE 7 THINGS YOU MUST KNOW

  1. Two requirements: a weak acid and its conjugate base, one proton apart.
  2. Weak means "hat on, not glued." The acid mostly keeps its proton but trades it back and forth. A strong acid throws it away for good, so its partner can't buffer.
  3. Acid uses up the base; base uses up the acid. A⁻ + H⁺ → HA and HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O, one for one.
  4. The head count sets the pH: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]).
  5. Home turf is pKa ± 1. Pick a buffer whose pKa is near the pH you want. There is no pH 7 rule.
  6. The size of the posse sets the capacity. More of both twins, more trouble the town can absorb.
  7. Blood is an open system. Lungs set PaCO₂ in minutes, kidneys set HCO₃⁻ over days, and that railroad is why bicarbonate works at 7.40.

THE COMMON MISTAKES

"The buffer cancels acid into nothing." No. The proton changes hands, or leaves on Ollie's head as water. And the pH still moves. It just moves very little.

"Weak means it holds the proton loosely." Backwards. A weak acid keeps its proton most of the time. What makes it useful is that the grip is reversible.

"Buffers hold things at pH 7." No. pKa and the ratio decide where the town sits.

"The lungs only kick in when the buffer is overwhelmed." No. They set PaCO₂ with every breath, all day long.

"Conjugate means opposite." No. It means one proton apart. Same twin, hat on or hat off.

MEMORY HOOK

"The hat is the proton. The Sheriff wears it but won't glue it. The head count sets the pH. The posse sets the capacity. And the railroad is your lungs and kidneys."

Hat on means acid (HA). Hat off means base (A⁻). An outlaw brings a hat, and the deputy takes it. Ollie steals a hat, and the sheriff goes bare.

ONE-LINE ANSWER TO "WHAT IS A BUFFER?"

A weak acid and its conjugate base, twins one proton apart, trading a hat back and forth so that any acid or base riding into town gets absorbed, while the pH, set by the pKa and the head count, barely flinches.

One last thing before you go. When you sit down for that exam and your mind goes blank, and it might, close your eyes and walk back into town. Count the hats. Check the head count. Ask yourself whether the train's still running. The chemistry will be standing right there behind the story, exactly where I hung it for you.
