A

Energy Foundations

⚡ What Is Energy?

  • Energy is the capacity to do work — not the ability to create energy (you cannot create or destroy energy; that is the First Law of Thermodynamics)
  • Energy exists in two broad forms: kinetic (energy of motion) and potential (stored energy, like the energy locked inside a glucose molecule)
  • Every process in a living cell — pumping ions, contracting muscle, synthesizing proteins — draws on the same energy pool
🏥
Clinical Connection When patients are NPO before surgery, their body taps into stored energy (glycogen, then fat). Understanding that energy is transformed, never created from nothing, explains why caloric intake matters — you cannot run a body on zero fuel.

🔁 Chemical Reactions: Reactants & Products

  • Reactants are what you start with (left of the arrow). Products are what you end up with (right of the arrow).
  • Reactants go through a reaction and are transformed into products
Cellular Respiration Equation C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ ⟶ 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP
  • In the equation above, glucose and oxygen are the reactants; CO₂, water, and ATP are the products
  • If a question asks you to identify a specific molecule's role, look at which side of the arrow it sits on
🧠
Memory Hook "Reactants REACT and transform into Products that are PRODUCED." Arrow points from start to finish, just like reading left to right.
🏥
Clinical Connection When you deliver supplemental O₂ to a patient, you are literally providing a reactant for cellular respiration — fueling every cell in their body.

🔗 Condensation (Dehydration Synthesis) Reactions

  • A condensation reaction forms a covalent bond between two monomers by removing a molecule of water
  • This is how the body builds polymers: linking amino acids into proteins, linking monosaccharides into polysaccharides, linking nucleotides into DNA
  • The reverse process is hydrolysis — breaking bonds by adding water
🧠
Memory Hook "CONDENSation = CONDENSes water out." Think of condensation on a cold glass — water leaves the system. Hydro-LYSIS = water LYSES (breaks) the bond.
B

Enzymes — Your Body's Chemical Workforce

🧸 Enzyme Structure

  • Enzymes are almost always proteins (not fats, not steroids, not hormones)
  • Because they are proteins, they can be denatured (unfolded) by extreme heat or extreme pH, which destroys their function
🏥
Clinical Connection This is exactly why malignant hyperthermia is a medical emergency during surgery — body temperature skyrockets, enzymes denature, and metabolic processes collapse.

⚒️ Enzyme Function

  • Enzymes are biological catalysts — they speed up specific chemical reactions without being changed or consumed
  • They are reusable: one enzyme molecule can catalyze the same reaction thousands of times
  • They are specific: each enzyme acts only on particular substrates
  • Enzymes do not require inhibitors to function; they lower the activation energy barrier all on their own
🧠
Memory Hook "Enzymes are like nurses on a unit — they HELP the reaction happen, they are SPECIFIC to their specialty, and they go home UNCHANGED at the end of the shift."

🔑 The Active Site

  • The active site is the specific region on an enzyme where the substrate binds and the reaction occurs
  • Only the correct substrate fits into the active site (like a key fitting a specific lock)
  • The active site's shape is determined by the enzyme's 3D protein structure
🧠
Memory Hook "Active Site = Action Site" — it is where the catalytic action happens. The substrate walks into the enzyme's office, sits in the right chair, and work gets done.

📈 Factors That Affect Enzyme Activity

  • Temperature — too high denatures the enzyme; too low slows the reaction
  • pH — each enzyme has an optimal pH (pepsin works best in the acidic stomach; trypsin works best in the basic small intestine)
  • Substrate concentration — more substrate means faster reaction, up to a saturation point where all active sites are occupied
❗
Key Concept Atmospheric pressure does NOT directly affect enzyme activity. The three factors that matter are temperature, pH, and substrate concentration. Don't confuse the environment around the cell with the chemistry inside the active site.
🧠
Memory Hook — "TPS" Temperature, PH, Substrate concentration — The three things that actually move the needle on enzyme rate. If it's not in TPS, it doesn't belong on the report.
🏥
Clinical Connection When you see elevated liver enzymes (AST, ALT) on a lab panel, it means liver cells are damaged and leaking their enzymes into the blood. The enzymes themselves haven't changed — their unexpected location is the diagnostic signal.

🔄 Feedback Inhibition

  • Feedback inhibition is a regulatory mechanism where the product of a metabolic pathway slows or stops the pathway that produces it
  • This is the cell's built-in thermostat — it prevents wasteful overproduction
  • The product binds to a regulatory site on an early enzyme in the pathway, shutting it down
🧠
Memory Hook "Too much product? SHUT IT DOWN." Imagine eating at an all-you-can-eat buffet — once your plate is overflowing, you wave the chef off. The product (full plate) inhibits the pathway (the chef cooking more).
🏥
Clinical Connection Statins work by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase, a key enzyme in cholesterol synthesis. This is the same logic as feedback inhibition — block the enzyme, reduce the product. Understanding this mechanism is understanding how drugs work at the molecular level.

Membrane Transport — The Cell's Border Control

🚪 Active vs. Passive Transport

FeatureActive TransportPassive Transport
ATP Required?YESNO
DirectionAgainst the gradient (low → high)Down the gradient (high → low)
AnalogyPushing a boulder uphillBall rolling downhill
ExamplesNa⁺/K⁺ pump, endocytosisDiffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion
🧠
Memory Hook "ACTIVE = ATP" (both start with A). "PASSIVE = FREE PASS" — no energy ticket needed.
🏥
Clinical Connection The Na⁺/K⁺ pump is essential for nerve impulse transmission and cardiac rhythm. Digoxin (a heart medication) works by inhibiting this pump. Understanding active transport = understanding cardiac pharmacology.

📦 Protein Trafficking: The Endomembrane Assembly Line

  • Proteins are synthesized on ribosomes attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER)
  • Once made, proteins are transported to the Golgi apparatus (Golgi body) for modification, sorting, and packaging
Ribosome → Rough ER → Golgi Apparatus → Final destination
  • Think of the rough ER as the factory floor and the Golgi as quality control & shipping
🏥
Clinical Connection Cystic fibrosis is caused by a defective CFTR protein that misfolds and gets stuck in the ER — the protein is made but never gets to where it needs to go. Protein trafficking errors cause real disease.

💧 Osmosis

  • Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a semipermeable membrane
  • Osmosis is a form of passive transport — it does NOT require ATP
  • Water moves from an area of low solute concentration toward high solute concentration (water chases solute)
🧠
Memory Hook "Water follows the salt." Wherever solute is more concentrated, water flows toward it to try to equalize. Think of it as water being pulled in by the crowd.

🧹 Tonicity & Its Effects on Cells

Solution TypeSolute ComparisonWater MovementCell Result
Hypertonic More solute outside Water leaves the cell Cell shrinks (crenation)
Hypotonic Less solute outside Water enters the cell Cell swells & may burst (lysis)
Isotonic Equal solute No net movement Cell stays the same
❗
Key Concept Tonicity is always described relative to the cell. A red blood cell containing 2% solute placed in a 1% solution is in a hypotonic environment (less solute outside than inside). Water rushes IN → cell expands and may burst.
🧠
Memory Hook "HYPER = the cell shrivels" (a hyper kid runs around so much they get dehydrated). "HYPO = the cell swells" (a hippo is BIG and bloated).
🏥
Clinical Connection Giving a hypotonic IV (like D5W after dextrose is metabolized) to a patient with cerebral edema could worsen brain swelling — water rushes into already-swollen brain cells. Conversely, hypertonic saline (3% NaCl) pulls water OUT of swollen tissue. Tonicity literally saves or takes lives in nursing.
D

ATP — The Universal Energy Currency

💰 ATP Structure & Energy Transfer

  • ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is the energy "currency" of the cell — virtually every energy-requiring process in the body spends ATP
  • Energy is released when ATP transfers a phosphate group to another molecule:
Energy Release from ATP ATP → ADP + Pᵢ + Energy
  • The transfer mechanism is a phosphate group — not heat, not adenosine, not the whole molecule
  • ADP can be recycled back to ATP by adding a phosphate group (requires energy input)
🧠
Memory Hook — "A Tiny Paycheck" ATP = A Tiny Paycheck. Cells spend it one phosphate at a time. Pop off a phosphate, release some cash (energy), and you're left with ADP (a depleted paycheck). Recharge it at the mitochondrial bank.
🏥
Clinical Connection Every muscle contraction, every nerve impulse, every active transport event costs ATP. In cyanide poisoning, the electron transport chain is blocked → ATP production halts → death within minutes. This is why understanding ATP is understanding survival.
E

Cellular Respiration — From Glucose to ATP

🏭 The Big Picture

  • Cellular respiration is the multi-step process cells use to extract energy from glucose and store it as ATP
  • It occurs in three major stages, each in a specific cellular location:
Glycolysis
Cytoplasm → Krebs Cycle
Mito. Matrix → ETC / Ox Phos
Inner Mito. Membrane
  • The mitochondria are the organelles responsible for aerobic respiration and ATP production in both plant and animal cells
🧠
Memory Hook — "Good Kings Eat" Glycolysis → Krebs cycle → Electron transport chain. Three stages, in order, every time.
🏥
Clinical Connection This is why we monitor both glucose AND oxygen in patients — they are the two essential inputs of the entire process. Without either one, cells cannot produce enough ATP to survive.

✂️ Glycolysis — Splitting Glucose

  • Occurs in the cytoplasm (does not require mitochondria)
  • Does NOT require oxygen — it can occur under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions
  • Input: 1 glucose molecule (6 carbons)
  • Outputs:
ProductAmountDetails
Pyruvate2 molecules3 carbons each (6 ÷ 2 = 3)
NADH2 moleculesElectron carriers for the ETC
ATPNet gain of 2(4 produced, 2 invested)
🧠
Memory Hook "Glyco-LYSIS = glucose gets LYSED (split)." One 6-carbon sugar is cut in half into two 3-carbon pyruvates. The number 2 dominates: 2 pyruvate, 2 NADH, 2 net ATP.
🏥
Clinical Connection Cancer cells often rely heavily on glycolysis even when oxygen IS present (the Warburg effect). This is the principle behind PET scans — radioactive glucose is injected, and tumors light up because they devour glucose at abnormal rates.

🔄 Citric Acid Cycle (Krebs Cycle)

  • Occurs in the mitochondrial matrix
  • Input: Acetyl-CoA (formed from pyruvate after glycolysis)
  • Outputs: NADH, FADH₂, CO₂, and a small amount of ATP
Krebs Cycle Summary Acetyl-CoA → NADH + FADH₂ + CO₂ + ATP
  • The CO₂ your patient exhales comes largely from this cycle — it is a metabolic waste product
  • The main purpose of the Krebs cycle is to generate electron carriers (NADH and FADH₂) that feed the electron transport chain
🧠
Memory Hook — "Krebs CRANKS out carriers" The Krebs cycle is the electron carrier factory. Its real output isn't ATP (that's just a bonus) — it's NADH and FADH₂, the loaded delivery trucks heading to the ETC.
🏥
Clinical Connection When you read PaCO₂ on an arterial blood gas panel, you are measuring a waste product of the Krebs cycle. Elevated CO₂ can mean hypermetabolism (the cycle is spinning too fast) or respiratory failure (the lungs aren't clearing it).

⚡ Oxidative Phosphorylation & the Electron Transport Chain

  • Occurs at the inner mitochondrial membrane
  • This is where the majority of ATP is produced
  • NADH and FADH₂ donate their electrons to a series of protein complexes (the "chain")
  • As electrons pass down the chain, energy is used to pump H⁺ ions, creating a gradient that drives ATP synthase
  • The final electron acceptor is oxygen (O₂) — this is why you breathe
  • Oxygen combines with the spent electrons and H⁺ ions to form water (H₂O)
❗
Key Concept O₂ is used at the very end of the electron transport chain — not at the beginning, not during the Krebs cycle, and not during glycolysis. Without O₂ to accept electrons at the finish line, the entire chain backs up and ATP production stops.
🧠
Memory Hook — "O₂ is the Final Destination" Electrons travel down the chain like passengers on a train. O₂ is the last stop. No O₂ at the station? The train can't unload, everything backs up, and the whole system shuts down.
🏥
Clinical Connection Cyanide blocks Complex IV of the ETC → electrons cannot reach O₂ → ATP production collapses → rapid cellular death. Treatment: hydroxocobalamin (Cyanokit) binds cyanide and reopens the chain. Carbon monoxide poisoning impairs O₂ delivery to the same chain — different mechanism, same downstream crisis.

💲 Total ATP Yield

  • From one molecule of glucose through complete aerobic respiration: approximately 36–38 ATP
StageATP Contribution
Glycolysis2 ATP (net) + 2 NADH
Pyruvate oxidation2 NADH
Krebs Cycle2 ATP + 6 NADH + 2 FADH₂
Electron Transport Chain~30–32 ATP (from all the NADH & FADH₂)
Grand Total~36–38 ATP
🧠
Memory Hook — "About 3 Dozen" One glucose → about 3 dozen ATPs. If someone says 8–12, that's only glycolysis. If someone says 125+ or 300+, that's science fiction.
🏥
Clinical Connection This is why aerobic exercise is more sustainable than sprinting — aerobic metabolism produces roughly 18× more ATP per glucose than anaerobic glycolysis alone (36 vs. 2). Your muscles can keep working when they have oxygen.
F

Fermentation — When Oxygen Isn't Available

🍺 Anaerobic Pathways

  • Fermentation occurs when oxygen is NOT available — these are anaerobic reactions
  • Purpose: regenerate NAD⁺ so that glycolysis can continue producing those 2 ATPs without the full aerobic pathway
  • There are two types:
FeatureAlcoholic FermentationLactate Fermentation
End productEthanol + CO₂Lactate
OrganismsYeast, some bacteriaHuman muscle cells, some bacteria
Real-world exampleBeer, wine, bread risingMuscle burn during intense exercise
Requires O₂?NONO
Starting moleculePyruvate (from glycolysis)Pyruvate (from glycolysis)
❗
Key Concept In humans, when oxygen is insufficient, the NADH produced during glycolysis is used to convert pyruvate into lactate. This is lactate fermentation — not alcoholic fermentation. Know which pathway humans use.
🧠
Memory Hook "ALcoholic fermentation = ALcohol (ethanol)." "Lactic fermentation = Lactate." The name literally gives you the product — the hardest part of biology is realizing the answer is in the name.
🏥
Clinical Connection — Lactic Acidosis When tissues don't receive enough oxygen (shock, sepsis, cardiac arrest), cells switch to anaerobic metabolism → lactate builds up → blood pH drops → lactic acidosis. An elevated serum lactate level is one of nursing's most critical lab values — it is a red flag for tissue hypoperfusion and can guide treatment decisions in the ICU. This is the biology behind the lab value.

📋 Module 3 Rapid-Fire Review

  • Energy = the capacity to do work
  • Enzymes = protein catalysts that speed reactions without being consumed
  • Active site = where the substrate binds on an enzyme
  • Enzyme rate affected by: Temperature, PH, Substrate concentration (NOT atmospheric pressure)
  • Feedback inhibition = product shuts down its own pathway
  • Condensation reaction = covalent bond formed between monomers (releases water)
  • Active transport = uses ATP; Passive = no ATP
  • Osmosis = passive diffusion of water across a semipermeable membrane
  • Hypotonic → cell swells/bursts; Hypertonic → cell shrinks
  • Rough ER makes proteins → Golgi modifies and ships them
  • ATP = energy currency; energy transferred via phosphate group
  • Glycolysis (cytoplasm): 1 glucose → 2 pyruvate + 2 NADH + 2 ATP (net)
  • Krebs cycle (mito matrix): Acetyl-CoA → NADH + FADH₂ + CO₂ + ATP
  • ETC (inner mito membrane): most ATP made here; O₂ is the final electron acceptor → H₂O
  • Total ATP yield from 1 glucose (aerobic): ~36–38 ATP
  • Fermentation = anaerobic (no O₂); alcoholic → ethanol; lactic → lactate
  • In humans without O₂: pyruvate → lactate
  • Mitochondria = the organelle where aerobic respiration produces ATP
