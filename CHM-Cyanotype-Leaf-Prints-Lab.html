Painting with Light: Cyanotype Leaf Prints

Two iron salts, a sheet of paper, a leaf and a UV lamp. Light reaches in, hands an electron to an iron atom, and a 180-year-old blue appears exactly where the leaf wasn't.

Multi-session chemistry project · 4 sessions over 1–2 weeks · Indoor UV-lamp exposure · Arizona College of Nursing

Why would a chemist make art with leaves?

Here's the thing that should make you a little giddy: the paper you coat today barely reacts to dim indoor light, but it wakes up under ultraviolet. Every photon of UV that lands on it can push an electron onto an iron(III) ion and turn it into iron(II). That tiny change, one electron, is the difference between a yellow-green stain that washes down the sink and an insoluble blue pigment that stays put for a century.

A leaf on the paper is a shadow mask. Where the leaf blocks the light, nothing happens, so the chemistry rinses away and the paper stays white. Where light gets through, Prussian blue forms. A thin leaf lets a little light through its blade but less through its veins, so you can get a map of the leaf's plumbing printed by redox chemistry.

Sir John Herschel invented this process in 1842. Anna Atkins used it in 1843 to make what is often called the first book illustrated with photographs, full of algae printed exactly the way you're about to print leaves. Engineers later used it to copy building plans, which is where the word blueprint comes from.

Learning objectives

  1. Explain photoreduction: how light energy drives the reduction Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺, and identify the oxidizing and reducing agents.
  2. Write the net ionic equation for Prussian blue formation and explain why its insolubility makes the image permanent.
  3. Prepare stock solutions from a mass/volume (% w/v) recipe and scale them to a class size.
  4. Design and run a controlled exposure-time experiment (test strip) and choose an optimal exposure from evidence.
  5. Test one variable of your choice (leaf type, development method or toning) with a control.
  6. Connect iron redox chemistry, light-driven reactions and Prussian blue to clinical practice.

Clinical connection: the same blue saves lives

Prussian blue isn't only an art pigment. Taken by mouth (brand name Radiogardase), it is an FDA-approved antidote for thallium poisoning and internal contamination with radioactive cesium. Its crystal lattice traps those ions in the gut so they leave in the stool instead of being reabsorbed. Patients are told their stool may turn blue, which you will understand much better after this lab.

The chemistry: one electron, one blue

Ferric ammonium citrate has no single formula. It is a mixture of iron(III)–citrate–ammonium complexes, which is why the recipe is given in grams and not in moles.

Step 1: Light drives a reduction (photoreduction)

Fe³⁺–citrate  +  hν (UV-A)  →  Fe²⁺  +  oxidized citrate  +  CO₂

The UV photon is absorbed by the iron–citrate complex. An electron moves from the citrate ligand onto the iron (a ligand-to-metal charge transfer). Iron is reduced (gains an electron). Citrate is oxidized and loses a carboxyl group as CO₂. Citrate is the reducing agent, but it can only act once light provides the energy. That's why the paper is stable in the dark.

Step 2: Fe²⁺ meets ferricyanide, and the blue precipitates

Fe²⁺(aq)  +  K⁺(aq)  +  [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻(aq)  →  KFe[Fe(CN)₆](s)
overall insoluble form:   Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃  (iron(III) hexacyanoferrate(II), 859.2 g/mol)

Inside Prussian blue, iron sits in two oxidation states at once, Fe(II) and Fe(III), linked by cyanide bridges. Light absorbed by an electron hopping between those two kinds of iron (an intervalence charge transfer) removes orange-red wavelengths, so what reaches your eye is an intense blue.

A dilute hydrogen peroxide dip speeds up the last oxidation and gives the final deep blue in seconds instead of the ~24 hours it takes in air. It is optional, and Session 3 compares the two.

Step 4 (Session 4): Bleaching and toning

Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃(s)  +  12 OH⁻  →  4 Fe(OH)₃(s)  +  3 [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻

Prussian blue is unstable in base, so a washing-soda (Na₂CO₃) bath fades the blue to a faint yellow iron(III) hydroxide image. Then tannins from black tea bind that iron(III), forming dark iron–tannate complexes. That is the same chemistry as medieval iron-gall ink. The image returns in browns, sepia or purple-black.

Clinical connection: the pathologist's Prussian blue

Hospital labs use Perls' Prussian blue stain, which reacts potassium ferrocyanide with ferric iron in tissue. It turns stored iron (hemosiderin) bright blue in bone marrow, liver and lung samples. It is used to assess iron stores and iron overload, for example in hemochromatosis or in patients who have had many transfusions. It's your reaction running in reverse roles: the iron is in the tissue, and the cyanide complex is in the stain.

Clinical connection: Fe²⁺ vs. Fe³⁺ at the bedside

Oral iron supplements such as ferrous sulfate supply Fe²⁺, because the gut absorbs ferrous iron far better than ferric iron. That is one reason vitamin C (a reducing agent) is often suggested with iron. It helps keep iron in the reduced Fe²⁺ form. Your citrate does the same job on the paper, powered by UV.

Safety & waste

Never acidify or heat potassium ferricyanide

Ferricyanide has low toxicity because its cyanide is locked tightly to iron. Strong acids or high heat can release hydrogen cyanide gas. Keep Solution B, the mixed sensitizer and all rinse water away from acids, and don't heat them. No vinegar or acid rinses in this lab.

UV lamp

UV-A can damage the eyes. Wear UV-blocking goggles, never look into the lamp, and switch it on only after the frame is in place under it. Keep skin out of the beam during exposures.

PPE

  • Goggles (UV-rated at the lamp)
  • Nitrile gloves when coating and rinsing
  • Lab coat or apron, since the solutions stain skin and clothes

Hazards

  • Ferric ammonium citrate: eye and skin irritant
  • K₃[Fe(CN)₆]: irritant; releases HCN with acid or heat
  • Na₂CO₃: eye irritant
  • 3% H₂O₂: eye irritant

Waste

  • Collect leftover concentrated sensitizer in the labeled waste container
  • Dispose of rinse water according to your institution's chemical waste policy
  • Gloves and paper towels with sensitizer go in the solid waste

Session 1: Press, mix, coat (in the dark)

Before Session 1 (2+ days ahead): Press your leaves

  1. Collect 4–6 leaves of different kinds: at least one thin or translucent leaf (fern, maple, ginkgo, Japanese maple) and one thick or waxy leaf (holly, magnolia). Why: thickness and translucency are your built-in variable for Session 3.
  2. Press them flat between paper towels inside a heavy book for at least 2 days. Why: a flat leaf makes full contact with the paper. Any gap lets light sneak under the edges and blurs them.

Class stock calculator

Assumes 5×7 in sheets plus 20% for spills and test strips. Mixed sensitizer is half A and half B.

Procedure

  1. Solution A: dissolve 25 g green ferric ammonium citrate in about 70 mL distilled water, then dilute to 100 mL. Store it in a labeled amber bottle. Why amber: the stock is light-sensitive too. It can grow mold within a few weeks, so make it fresh for the project.
  2. Solution B: dissolve 10 g potassium ferricyanide in about 70 mL distilled water, then dilute to 100 mL. Store it in a labeled amber bottle.
  3. Dim the room. Close the blinds and use only normal indoor lighting, away from windows. No UV lamp on anywhere in the room.
  4. Mix the sensitizer: combine equal volumes of A and B in a small beaker, enough for this session only. Why: the mixed sensitizer starts to degrade within hours. The separate stocks last much longer.
  5. Label the back of each sheet of watercolor paper in pencil: name, date, sheet number.
  6. Coat: brush on a thin, even layer with a foam brush, first left–right and then top–bottom. Coat 4 full sheets plus 1 strip about 2 × 8 in for the test strip. Why thin: puddles stay sticky, dry blotchy and wash out unevenly.
  7. Dry in the dark box (a closed cabinet, drawer or cardboard box) overnight. The coating should look an even yellow-green.
  8. Wash the brushes and beakers with plenty of water. Close the stock bottles and store them in the dark.
Decision point: Does the dry coating look streaky or blotchy? → Recoat a new sheet more thinly. Is any sheet already blue before exposure? → It was exposed to light, so discard it and check your dark box for leaks.

Clinical connection: medications that ban sunlight

Your paper must be protected from light, and so must some patients and drugs. Drug-induced photosensitivity (doxycycline, sulfonamides, amiodarone, some thiazides) can make normal UV exposure cause severe sunburn. Light-sensitive IV drugs such as nitroprusside are given with protective covers on the bag and tubing for the same reason your stock bottles are amber.

Session 2: The test strip (finding your exposure time)

Every UV lamp, distance and batch of paper is different. Instead of guessing, run a controlled experiment: one strip, several exposure times, one rinse. Then let the strip tell you the answer.

  1. Fix your lamp at a measured distance (record it, e.g. 15 cm) and keep it the same for every exposure in this project.
  2. Place the test strip coated side up on cardboard. Lay one leaf across its full length, then cover with a clean glass or acrylic sheet.
  3. Cover the whole strip with an opaque card. Turn the lamp on and start the timer below.
  4. At each beep step, slide the card back to uncover the next section. The first section uncovered gets the longest exposure.
  5. Rinse the strip in a tray of cool water for about 5 minutes, changing the water until it runs clear. Let it dry.
  6. Choose the shortest time that gives a deep, saturated blue background and a crisp white leaf. Record it in the Data tab.

Reading a strip: a section that is pale or chalky blue after rinsing was underexposed. When blue starts to creep under the leaf edges, that section was overexposed. Unrinsed, a well-exposed area looks grey-blue to bronze. The color you see before rinsing is not the final color.

Session 3: Leaf prints and your variable

Pick one variable to test

Option A: Leaf structure

Print a thin leaf and a thick leaf side by side at the same exposure. Which one shows veins, and why?

Option B: Development

Make two identical prints. Rinse one in water only, and give the other a water rinse plus a peroxide dip. Compare them right away and after 24 h.

Option C: Exposure

Print the same leaf at 0.5×, 1× and 2× your chosen exposure. How does detail inside the leaf change?

Procedure

  1. Arrange your leaves on a coated sheet, coated side up. Press them flat under the glass or acrylic sheet. Why: firm contact gives sharp edges.
  2. Expose under the lamp at your Session 2 time, at the same distance.
  3. Remove the leaves (they may be slightly stained, so don't eat them, and discard them in the solid waste). Rinse the print in cool water for about 5 minutes until the water runs clear.
  4. Optional peroxide dip: about 15 mL of 3% H₂O₂ per 1 L of water, 20–30 seconds, then a final water rinse. Watch the blue deepen instantly.
  5. Dry the prints flat on paper towels, image side up. Photograph each print right away and again after 24 h for your Data tab.
Decision point: Blue under the leaf edges? → Contact was poor or the exposure too long. Press harder, or shorten the time. Whole print pale after rinsing? → Underexposed, or the coating was too thin. Lengthen the time.

Clinical connection: light as a treatment

Neonatal jaundice is treated with phototherapy. Blue light (about 460–490 nm) changes bilirubin in the baby's skin into water-soluble isomers that can be excreted without needing the liver to conjugate them. As in your print, a photon reshapes a molecule, and that change determines whether it dissolves and washes away.

Session 4: Bleach, tone and gallery walk

Now you'll take a finished print apart chemically and rebuild it in a different color. Use your least favorite print or a spare.

  1. Tea bath: steep 4–6 black tea bags in 500 mL of hot water for 10 minutes, then let it cool to room temperature.
  2. Bleach: dissolve about 10 g of sodium carbonate (washing soda) in 1 L of water. Immerse the print and watch until the blue fades to a pale yellow (usually 1–5 minutes). Pull it out sooner if you want split blue/brown tones. Why: OH⁻ from carbonate breaks Prussian blue down to Fe(OH)₃ (the equation is in the Chemistry tab).
  3. Rinse the print in water for 2 minutes.
  4. Tone: soak the print in the cooled tea for 30 minutes to several hours. Check it every 15 minutes. Longer soaks give darker browns and purple-blacks.
  5. Rinse and dry flat. Note that the paper itself also picks up a tea tint.
  6. Gallery walk: display your best print, your test strip and your toned print with a 3-sentence caption: what variable you tested, what you saw, and the chemistry that explains it.

Clinical connection: tannins bind iron in you too

The tannins that grab iron(III) in your toning bath also bind dietary iron in the gut. Tea and coffee taken with meals can reduce non-heme iron absorption. That's why patients on iron supplements are often advised to separate their doses from tea and coffee.

Quick reference

Recipe card

  • A: 25 g green ferric ammonium citrate → 100 mL
  • B: 10 g K₃[Fe(CN)₆] → 100 mL
  • Mix A + B 1 : 1 right before coating
  • About 2 mL mixed per 5×7 sheet

Light rules

  • Coat and dry in dim indoor light
  • Dry in the dark box overnight
  • Expose only under the UV lamp
  • Keep the same lamp distance all project long

Color guide

  • Yellow-green: unexposed coating
  • Grey-blue/bronze: exposed, not rinsed
  • Bright blue: rinsed
  • Deep Prussian blue: after peroxide or 24 h
  • Brown/purple-black: tea-toned

Key equations

  • Fe³⁺ + e⁻ → Fe²⁺ (driven by UV, citrate gives the e⁻)
  • Fe²⁺ + K⁺ + [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ → KFe[Fe(CN)₆](s)
  • Base: Prussian blue + OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃
