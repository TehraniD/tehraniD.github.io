Crystal Growing Semester Project

Monoammonium Phosphate (MAP) — From Solution to Structure

CHM-130 · Fundamentals of Chemistry
Introduction

On your very first day of chemistry, you will prepare a supersaturated solution and set a crystal in motion. Over the next 7–8 weeks, that crystal will grow — atom by atom, layer by layer — into a structure you can hold in your hand. Along the way, you will see solubility, supersaturation, nucleation, and crystal habit stop being vocabulary words and become things you can watch happen in real time.

The compound you will grow is monoammonium phosphate (MAP), chemical formula NH4H2PO4. It is the same substance used in ABC fire extinguishers and in agricultural fertilizers. It dissolves readily in hot water and crystallizes into beautiful tetragonal prisms as the solution cools — exactly the same mechanism that forms kidney stones in the human body.

🏥 Clinical Connection — Why Nurses Should Understand Crystallization

  • Kidney stones (nephrolithiasis): Calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate crystals form in urine when concentration exceeds solubility — the identical supersaturation mechanism you will observe in this lab.
  • Gout: Monosodium urate crystallizes in synovial fluid of joints when serum uric acid exceeds ~6.8 mg/dL (its solubility limit at 37 °C).
  • Gallstones: Cholesterol crystallization in bile — supersaturation plus nucleation on mucin glycoproteins.
  • Drug polymorphism: The same drug molecule can form different crystal structures (polymorphs) with different dissolution rates. Ritonavir's market recall in 1998 happened because an unexpected polymorph formed during manufacturing, rendering the capsules therapeutically inactive.
  • IV crystalloid fluids: The clinical term "crystalloid" (Normal Saline, Lactated Ringer's) literally derives from substances that can form crystals — contrasted with "colloids" that cannot.
Key Concepts

Solubility describes the maximum amount of a solute that will dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature. For MAP, solubility increases steeply with temperature:

Temperature (°C)Solubility (g MAP per 100 g H₂O)What It Means
1029Cold tap water
2037Room temperature — your lab
2541Warm day
4056Warm bath
6083Hot but touchable
80122Near boiling — preparation temp
100177Boiling point of water

Supersaturation occurs when a hot, concentrated solution cools: it now contains more dissolved solute than the solvent can hold at the lower temperature. The excess must come out of solution as solid crystals. The degree of supersaturation controls what happens next:

  • High supersaturation → many tiny crystals (rapid nucleation, small crystal size)
  • Low supersaturation → fewer, larger, higher-quality crystals (slow growth, better structure)

In Week 1, you will use high supersaturation to generate many small seed crystals. In Week 2, you will select the best seed and move it into a low-supersaturation environment where it can grow slowly into a large, well-formed crystal over the remaining weeks.

Nucleation is the event where dissolved molecules first organize into a tiny ordered cluster — the "birth" of a crystal. Nucleation can be homogeneous (spontaneous) or heterogeneous (triggered by a surface — a scratch on glass, a dust particle, or a seed crystal you deliberately introduce).

Crystal system: MAP crystallizes in the tetragonal system (a = b ≠ c, all angles 90°). Expect elongated prisms or needle-like habits, especially if supersaturation is high.

⚠️ Safety Precautions

  • Goggles required whenever handling MAP solution or hot water.
  • MAP solutions are mildly acidic (pH ≈ 3.8–4.4) — avoid eye contact. In case of splash, flush with water for 15 minutes and notify your instructor.
  • Hot water hazard: Solutions are prepared at ≥ 80 °C. Handle beakers with heat-resistant gloves or beaker tongs. Never carry open beakers of hot solution.
  • MAP is low-toxicity (used in food-grade fertilizers), but do not ingest. Wash hands thoroughly after each session.
  • Disposal: MAP solution can be poured down the drain with running water — it is not a hazardous waste. Leftover solid MAP can go in the regular trash.
  • If crystals break, the fragments are not sharp in the way glass is, but dispose of small pieces rather than leaving them loose.

Week 1 — Solution Preparation & Nucleation (First Day of Class) ▶

Goal: Dissolve MAP in hot water to create a supersaturated solution. As it cools undisturbed, many small seed crystals will form over the next 2–5 days.

💡 The Chemistry

At 80 °C, water can dissolve ~122 g of MAP per 100 g. At room temperature (~22 °C), it can only hold ~37 g. By dissolving ~55–60 g in 100 mL of hot water, then cooling, you force ~18–23 g to crystallize out. This mass becomes hundreds of tiny seed crystals coating the bottom and sides of your container.

  1. Put on safety goggles. Label your beaker/jar with your name, section, and date using masking tape.
  2. Using the digital scale, weigh out 55 g of MAP powder (use your kit's bag first; supplement with bulk MAP if needed). Record the exact mass in your data table.
  3. Measure 100 mL of water in your beaker. Heat on a hot plate (or use pre-heated water from the instructor) until the temperature reaches 80 °C ± 5 °C. Record the temperature.
  4. Slowly add the 55 g of MAP to the hot water while stirring continuously with a glass rod. Stir for at least 2 minutes until all powder is completely dissolved. The solution should be perfectly clear — no visible grains. If any powder remains, continue heating gently and stirring.
  5. Optional: Add a small pinch of the colored seeding mixture from your 4M kit and stir to dissolve. This adds color to your crystals.
  6. Remove from heat. Do not stir once you remove it. Allow the solution to cool undisturbed.
  7. Cover the opening loosely with plastic wrap or a coffee filter secured with a rubber band (to keep out dust but allow slow evaporation).
  8. Place the beaker in your assigned shelf location. Do not move it until Week 2.
  9. In your observation log, sketch the solution and record: initial mass of MAP, volume of water, dissolution temperature, and the time you set it to cool.

Week 2 — Seed Crystal Selection & Growth Solution Setup ▶

Goal: Choose the best seed crystal from Week 1's crop. Prepare a fresh, mildly supersaturated growing solution. Suspend the seed crystal in the new solution to begin controlled growth.

  1. Observe your beaker. You should see a mass of small crystals on the bottom and possibly the sides. Record your observations and sketch what you see.
  2. Carefully pour the remaining liquid (the "mother liquor") into a separate container and set it aside — do not discard it. You will use it later.
  3. Select your seed crystal. Examine the crystal crop and choose the single best specimen:
    • Look for a crystal that is clear (not cloudy or milky)
    • Look for well-defined faces and edges (symmetrical shape)
    • Choose one that is approximately 3–5 mm in its longest dimension
    • Avoid crystals that are fused together in clusters
  4. Tie or loop a piece of monofilament fishing line around your seed crystal. If it is too small to tie, use a tiny dab of clear nail polish to glue the line to the crystal and let it dry for 5 minutes.
  5. Tie the other end of the line to the center of a pencil. Adjust the length so that when the pencil rests across the top of your beaker/jar, the crystal hangs in the center of the solution — not touching the bottom or sides.
  6. Prepare the growing solution: Weigh 45 g of fresh bulk MAP. Heat 100 mL of water to 60 °C. Dissolve the MAP completely (all powder gone, clear solution).
  7. Let the growing solution cool to approximately 30–35 °C (still warm to the touch, but not hot). This is critical — if it is too hot, it will dissolve your seed crystal.
  8. Gently lower your seed crystal into the warm solution. Rest the pencil across the rim. The crystal should be fully submerged and suspended freely.
  9. Cover loosely. Return to your shelf location. Record seed crystal dimensions (length × width × height in mm) and mass if possible.

🏥 Clinical Connection — Seeding & Kidney Stones

  • In the kidney, a tiny crystal fragment or a cellular debris particle can serve as a nucleation site — exactly like your seed crystal. Once a small crystal exists, the supersaturated urine deposits more solute onto it, making it larger.
  • Clinical interventions like increased hydration and citrate supplementation work by reducing urinary supersaturation — the same concept you are exploiting by using a milder solution in Week 2 compared to Week 1.

Week 3 — First Growth Check & Observation ▶

Goal: Measure crystal growth. Assess solution condition. Remove any parasitic crystals.

  1. Without removing the crystal from the solution, observe it through the glass. Note any visible growth, changes in shape, or cloudiness in the solution.
  2. Gently lift the pencil to raise the crystal just above the solution surface. Work quickly — the wet crystal surface can dissolve slightly in air if left exposed too long (> 30 seconds).
  3. Measure the crystal dimensions (L × W × H) in millimeters using your ruler. Record in your data table.
  4. If you can weigh the crystal without detaching it from the line, do so. Otherwise, estimate mass from dimensions.
  5. Check for parasitic crystals: Look for small unwanted crystals growing on the fishing line, on the pencil, or on the bottom of the jar. If present, carefully remove them — they steal dissolved MAP from your growing crystal. You can scrape them off with a clean spoon or fingertip.
  6. Check solution clarity. If the solution is becoming cloudy or many tiny crystals are forming spontaneously, the supersaturation may be too high. Add 5–10 mL of warm water and stir gently to re-dissolve the extras.
  7. Lower crystal back into solution. Re-cover. Return to shelf.
  8. Sketch the crystal from two angles. Record all data in your observation log.

💡 What If My Crystal Dissolved?

This means your growing solution was unsaturated at room temperature — it could hold more MAP than was present, so it pulled MAP off your crystal. This happens if the solution was too warm when you added the seed, or if the room is warmer than expected. Fix: Dissolve an additional 5–10 g of MAP in a small amount of warm water, let it cool to room temperature, then add it to your growing jar.

Week 4 — Refeeding the Solution ▶

Goal: Replenish the supersaturation that your growing crystal has consumed. This is the most important maintenance step for continued growth.

  1. Measure and record crystal dimensions and mass (same protocol as Week 3).
  2. Remove the crystal from the solution. Place it on a clean paper towel. Loosely cover it with plastic wrap to protect it.
  3. Prepare the refeed: In a separate container, dissolve 15–20 g of fresh MAP in 30 mL of water heated to 60 °C. Stir until clear.
  4. Let the refeed solution cool to 30 °C.
  5. Carefully pour the refeed into your growing jar. Stir gently to mix.
  6. Check for any rogue crystals on the jar walls or bottom. Remove them — a clean jar with a single suspended crystal gives the best results.
  7. Re-suspend your crystal in the refreshed solution. Cover. Return to shelf.
  8. Record the amount of MAP added and all observations.

Week 5 — Growth Measurement & Crystal Habit Analysis ▶

Goal: Measure growth. Analyze crystal shape (habit) and begin examining crystal quality. Remove parasitic crystals.

  1. Measure and record crystal dimensions and mass (same protocol).
  2. Examine the crystal habit. MAP crystals are tetragonal — the ideal crystal has four equivalent prismatic faces along the long axis and flat termination faces on the ends. Sketch your crystal and label any faces you can identify.
  3. Note any inclusions (bubbles, cloudiness, or trapped solution inside the crystal). These form when growth is too rapid or the crystal was disturbed during growth.
  4. Check for and remove parasitic crystals on the thread and jar surfaces.
  5. If growth has slowed noticeably since Week 4, this is normal — the supersaturation is getting consumed. You will refeed again in Week 6.
  6. Lower crystal back. Cover. Return to shelf. Record all observations.
Week 6 — Second Refeeding & Color Band Experiment ▶

Goal: Refeed the solution for continued growth. Optionally, add food coloring to create a visible growth band inside the crystal — direct evidence that the crystal grew layer by layer.

  1. Measure and record crystal dimensions and mass.
  2. Remove crystal from solution (same careful protocol as Week 4).
  3. Prepare and add a refeed solution (same recipe as Week 4: 15–20 g MAP in 30 mL water at 60 °C, cooled to 30 °C).
  4. Color band experiment (optional but recommended): Add 2–3 drops of food coloring (any color) to the refreshed solution and stir to distribute evenly. The crystal will incorporate the dye into its new growth layers, creating a colored band visible inside the otherwise clear crystal. This is a dramatic demonstration that the crystal adds material to its outer surface, layer by layer.
  5. Clean parasitic crystals. Re-suspend your crystal. Cover. Return to shelf.
  6. Record the color used and all measurements.

🏥 Clinical Connection — Growth Zones

  • The colored band in your crystal is analogous to growth lines in kidney stones. When urologists section a kidney stone, they can see concentric growth rings that record changes in urine composition over time — periods of dehydration, dietary changes, or medication adjustments.
  • In pharmacology, growth zones in drug crystals can trap different amounts of impurity, affecting drug purity and dissolution rate.
Week 7 — Final Growth Measurement & Crystal Harvest ▶

Goal: Take final measurements. Remove the crystal from solution permanently. Dry and preserve it.

  1. Measure final crystal dimensions (L × W × H) in millimeters.
  2. Carefully lift the crystal from the solution. Pat gently with a clean, dry paper towel. Do not rub — you may scratch the crystal faces.
  3. Weigh the dry crystal on the digital scale. Record the final mass.
  4. Carefully cut the fishing line, leaving a small tag if desired.
  5. Preservation: MAP crystals are stable at room temperature and humidity. If your lab is humid, a thin coat of clear nail polish or clear acrylic spray will protect the crystal from absorbing moisture. Apply a thin, even coat and let dry.
  6. Place your crystal in the 4M kit display case for presentation.
  7. Dispose of the remaining solution by pouring down the drain with running water.
  8. Record final data and complete your observation log.
Week 8 — Data Analysis & Presentation ▶

Goal: Analyze your growth data. Calculate growth rate. Compare results across the class. Submit your final lab report.

  1. Calculate total mass gained: Final crystal mass − estimated seed crystal mass = mass deposited from solution.
  2. Calculate average growth rate: Divide total mass gained by the number of days in solution. Express as mg/day or g/week.
  3. Plot your growth curve: Create a graph with Week number on the x-axis and crystal mass (or longest dimension) on the y-axis. Connect data points to visualize the growth trajectory. Was it linear? Did it slow down between refeedings? Did it accelerate after a refeed?
  4. Class comparison: Record your final crystal mass and longest dimension on the class data board. Look for patterns:
    • Which shelf locations produced the largest crystals? (Temperature gradients in the room)
    • Did students who refeeded more frequently get larger crystals?
    • Did colored crystals grow at different rates than uncolored ones?
  5. Reflection questions (answer in your lab notebook):
    • How does the mechanism of crystal growth in this lab mirror the formation of kidney stones in a patient?
    • What clinical interventions disrupt the crystallization process that leads to kidney stones, and which step of crystallization does each intervention target (supersaturation, nucleation, or growth)?
    • If you could repeat this experiment, what would you change and why?
