Background

The history and chemistry behind aspirin synthesis

A Brief History

As early as the 4th century B.C.E., a tea made from boiling the bark of the white willow tree (Salix alba) was used to reduce fever. The active compound, salicin, was eventually isolated and found to reduce fevers, pain, and inflammation.

The problem? Salicylic acid caused severe stomach irritation and bleeding. It wasn't until 1899 when Felix Hoffman, working for Bayer, developed a modern synthesis by acetylating salicylic acid - creating aspirin, which is far gentler on the stomach.

How NSAIDs Work

Aspirin and other nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) block cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes. COX enzymes catalyze the production of prostaglandins - hormone-like molecules that produce fever, swelling, and sensitize pain receptors.

By blocking COX, aspirin reduces pain, fever, and inflammation. It also inhibits platelet aggregation, which is why low-dose aspirin is used to prevent blood clots.

The Reaction

Aspirin is synthesized by reacting salicylic acid with acetic anhydride in the presence of a sulfuric acid catalyst. This is an esterification reaction - the hydroxyl group (-OH) on salicylic acid reacts with acetic anhydride to form an ester bond.

Balanced Equation
C7H6O3 + C4H6O3 ⟶ C9H8O4 + C2H4O2
Salicylic Acid (138.12 g/mol) + Acetic Anhydride (102.09 g/mol) → Aspirin (180.16 g/mol) + Acetic Acid (60.05 g/mol)
Catalyst: H2SO4 (concentrated sulfuric acid)
Molar ratio - Salicylic acid : Aspirin = 1 : 1

Why Acetic Anhydride Instead of Acetic Acid?

The reaction with acetic acid is reversible and slow. Using acetic anhydride drives the reaction forward - it reacts faster and produces a higher yield because the reaction is essentially irreversible.
